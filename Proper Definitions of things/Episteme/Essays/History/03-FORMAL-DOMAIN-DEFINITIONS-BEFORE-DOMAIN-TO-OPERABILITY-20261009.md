# Formal Domain Definitions — Working Definition

**Status:** Working definition — revised 9 October 2026.

**Purpose:** To develop our full conceptual account of fields, domains, their internal organization and their integration, and on that foundation to explain what it means to define a domain formally. The account distinguishes existing domains, their participation in productive undertakings, and the deliberate constitution of domains around intended capabilities. It then develops the objects, meanings, relationships and possible work that an adequate formal definition must make explicit. These definitions are work in their own right; a later essay may draw upon them.

The explanation begins with the subject already present in knowledge and practice. It does not reserve the word *domain* for something newly designed around a productive purpose. Such design is an important further construction whose meaning depends on understanding fields and domains first. The earlier sketches supply useful conceptual relationships to retain and deepen; particular claims and generalizations still require assessment.

The existing [Abstography definition](<../Abstography/01-ABSTOGRAPHY-WORKING-DEFINITION.md>) is an established reference for this work and must remain untouched. This document develops a related subject in depth; it does not redefine Abstography or reduce it to a later execution layer.

**Explanatory sequence:** §§1–2 establish fields, domains and the relationship between their knowledge and their explicit constitution. §3 explains their integration in productive work. §4 introduces deliberately constituted domains and the meaning of an arbitrarily specified capability. §5 then defines formal domain definition across those cases. §§6–15 deepen its constituent responsibilities and adequacy questions. §§16–18 connect the account to the other definitions and identify further work.

## 1. Fields and domains before deliberate capability design

### 1.1 What a field is

**A field is an organized region of knowledge, inquiry or practice, identified through connected subjects, characteristic questions, concepts, methods and forms of activity.** Its organization makes some objects and differences significant, provides ways of investigating or working with them, and supplies relations through which results can be understood and used.

A field is therefore more than the documents in which its knowledge is recorded or the institution in which it is taught. Those can carry and develop the field; its intellectual and practical organization concerns what is studied or done, which distinctions matter and how its contributions relate. Its subjects may be empirical, formal, interpretive, constructive or combinations of these.

A developed field can contain different approaches, incomplete accounts and disagreements. Its existence does not presuppose one finished theory. The identity and changing boundaries of a field require further explanation where they become consequential; the initial point is that there is already an organization to understand, not an empty space awaiting a productive designer.

### 1.2 What a domain is

**A domain is a distinguishable, relatively bounded region of subjects, knowledge or activity, understood through the objects, relationships, questions and practices that give it a particular scope and internal organization.** In relation to a broader field, a domain usually identifies a more delimited region within it. Its boundary can concern a kind of subject, a family of phenomena or problems, a mode of inquiry or a practice.

A domain need not have been deliberately assembled around a new capability. It can already exist through the development of knowledge and practice, with its own concepts, questions and internal distinctions. Nor must it already possess a complete formal definition to count as a domain. Having an organized subject and giving that organization an adequate explicit account are different achievements.

The boundary makes the scope intelligible; it need not be impermeable or permanently settled. A domain can depend on knowledge developed elsewhere, share objects with neighboring domains or contain disagreements about its own limits. More exact criteria of individuation must be developed when the work requires them.

### 1.3 Their distinction is relative to the organization being considered

*Field* and *domain* do not name two permanently separate kinds of entity. Their use often expresses a difference of scope and level of consideration. A region can be treated as a domain in one wider account and as a field when its own internal regions are being examined.

Materials science can be considered a field relative to regions such as metallurgy, ceramics and composite materials. Within a broader account of engineering, materials science can instead be treated as a contributing domain, whose own internal domains remain distinguishable. Biology, microbiology and genetics similarly illustrate differences of scope without requiring that every disciplinary relationship fit one strict tree.

Nesting is one relationship; overlap and contribution are others. Saying that architectural work draws upon several engineering domains does not make all engineering a taxonomic subfield of architecture. We must distinguish a region's place in an organization of knowledge from its role in a particular undertaking. That distinction will matter when we turn to industrial integration (§3).

### 1.4 Their existing internal organization

Fields and domains can already possess substantial rational organization, even where that organization is not collected into one formal account. *Rational organization* here concerns the relations through which subjects, reasons, methods and results can be understood and examined. It does not certify every inherited claim or require all disagreements to have been resolved.

| Constituent of an existing field or domain | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| Objects and distinctions | What the work concerns and which differences are consequential. |
| Concepts and meanings | How those subjects and relationships are understood. |
| Questions and problems | What is investigated, explained, compared, designed or otherwise worked upon. |
| Theories, models and explanations | Accounts of relationships, behavior, possibilities and limits. |
| Methods and instruments | Ways of obtaining, reasoning with or transforming relevant material. |
| Results and their uses | What counts as an answer, finding, design or other achievement, and how it enters further work. |
| Grounds and standards of assessment | How claims, performances and disagreements are evaluated, and what counts as progress, failure or a need for revision. |
| Relations to other domains | What is borrowed, shared, supplied or reinterpreted across boundaries. |

These constituents have different forms and degrees of development in different subjects. Their relationships make the region more than a list of topics. A method concerns particular objects under particular premises; a result answers a question at a scope; a concept can determine which differences an instrument needs to preserve.

### 1.5 The subject, its knowledge and its definition

We must distinguish **what a domain concerns**, **the knowledge and practices through which it is approached**, and **the account defining that organization**. A subject's empirical properties are not created by writing a definition of them. An inherited account can be incomplete or mistaken while still referring to a real subject. In a deliberately constructed formal domain, some objects and laws are instead constituted by definition; those cases require their appropriate interpretation.

These distinctions let us ask how a domain is organized before asking how to formalize it. They also prevent us from treating an inadequate account as proof that there was no domain or knowledge there in the first place.

## 2. Existing knowledge and the explicit constitution of a domain

### 2.1 Formal knowledge within a domain

A domain can contain exact equations, proofs, models, classifications and procedures alongside explanatory material and practical knowledge. These contributions can be highly developed without collectively supplying a full account of how their subjects, premises and uses fit together.

**Formal knowledge within a domain is not the same achievement as a formal definition of the domain.** A calculation can be correct for the configuration supplied to it while leaving open how that configuration was identified, why the selected assumptions apply and what a later activity may conclude from the result. An exact local answer does not automatically define those connecting relationships.

The distinction is between the scope of a formal contribution and the organization within which it becomes meaningful. It does not diminish the contribution. Some connecting work may itself already be explicit and formal; the question is which relationships are actually supplied and which remain dependent on further interpretation or construction.

### 2.2 Explanation and competent practice carry real structure

Explanations can supply a model's meaning, scope and applicability, while practical experience helps determine which situation is present and which differences matter. Those are substantive parts of how knowledge works, not dispensable decoration around the equations.

The older draft's *human compiler* analogy points to this contribution. A competent practitioner may resolve the relevant object and context, assemble inputs, identify an exception, connect different representations and determine what a result can support. Sometimes the practitioner recovers an established relationship; sometimes the situation requires a new judgment or construction. Those acts should not be treated as identical.

This is an analogy for interpretive and constructive work, not a literal architecture of cognition. At the scale of a productive undertaking, the work can be distributed among people, methods, institutions and technical instruments. Its success does not depend on one person holding the entire organization in mind.

The definitional question is which parts of that organization have been given an adequate explicit account. Where important connections remain carried only through competent practice, the domain can function while a formal representation of it remains incomplete. Making the connection explicit is a further achievement, not a retrospective creation of all the knowledge that made the practice possible.

### 2.3 The connected account, not merely a larger collection

Adding more local results does not by itself define their joint organization. The account may still need to explain when two presentations concern the same object, what a translation preserves, how assumptions interact or whether the conclusion of one construction supplies what another requires.

Two specialties can concern the same physical component through different models and relationships. Their accounts may be complementary rather than competing. Integration must explain what is shared, what is context-specific and how the contributions can be used together. A common name or a connection between documents does not perform that work.

This identifies a problem to investigate within a particular domain, not a blanket verdict that all contemporary domains have the same deficiencies. Their formal development must be assessed at the relationships actually being claimed.

## 3. How domains participate in productive undertakings

### 3.1 Selection and integration already occur in practice

A productive undertaking requires contributions from the domains relevant to what it is trying to accomplish. It selects particular objects, methods, models and constraints; determines the depth at which they are needed; and establishes dependencies among their contributions.

That organization can already exist in practical and institutional form. People, technical systems, standards and working arrangements can coordinate it without possessing one fully formalized account of the whole. Deliberate formal domain design is therefore not the invention of integration itself. It seeks a more explicit constitution of what the integration requires and can make possible.

The important distinction is between **membership in a field or domain** and **participation in this undertaking**. A result can belong to one region of knowledge while supplying several different productive activities. Only part of a domain may be relevant to one undertaking; that does not redefine the whole source domain as the part selected.

### 3.2 Different purposes require different organizations

Consider, as an explanatory comparison, the design and construction of a detached house and of a skyscraper. Both can draw on materials, structural design, building services and other domains. Their difference is not adequately expressed by saying that one simply uses more of the same knowledge.

For a particular tall-building undertaking, the relation among wind response, structural behavior, enclosure movement and other requirements can demand a different joint account from that of a particular low-rise undertaking. Construction stages may themselves require distinct configurations and analyses. Which dependencies apply is part of the actual engineering account, not fixed by this example.

The point is the change in **organization**: which subjects become consequential, which models apply, what each contribution must supply to another and how a local change affects the intended whole. The comparison illustrates why a list of participating specialties cannot substitute for their integration. It does not impose a universal complexity ranking on all buildings.

### 3.3 The productive whole has relationships of its own

We can call such a connected organization a *productive domain ecology*: participating domains or parts of domains together with the relations through which they contribute to an undertaking. This is a working descriptive term, not yet a prescribed mathematical structure.

Its dependencies can expose additional work. If one result requires assumptions about another subsystem, those assumptions and the means of establishing them become part of the required account. A whole can consequently require connecting objects, comparisons or constraints that do not belong to any isolated specialty as previously described.

Dependency closure, where that term is used, concerns supplying the premises and connections required for the claimed work. It does not mean incorporating all knowledge from every contributing field. The relevant depth follows the actual responsibility and its justified dependencies.

A particular undertaking can assemble these relationships for its own occurrence. A standing capability can also persist through people, institutions and technical arrangements. To give that capability an adequate formal account, we must explain which relations persist across changing occurrences and how the relevant organization is formed again. Deliberate constitution can make this organization explicit and reusable, while also developing relationships and powers that the inherited arrangements do not yet supply.

### 3.4 Terrain as a possible wider account

The older sketch uses *Terrain* for a wider organization of fields and domains from which different productive integrations can be developed. Its useful role would be to distinguish this wider field of available relationships from the particular domain ecology formed for one capability.

If retained, the concept will need its own account of relevance, boundary and change. Being irrelevant to a present undertaking is different from being permanently unavailable to every future undertaking. A Terrain should not silently turn today's selection into a universal exclusion. The concept remains a candidate for development, not a prerequisite for understanding field, domain or formal definition.

## 4. Deliberately constituted domains and arbitrarily specified capabilities

Only on the preceding foundation do we introduce the more specialized construction: **a deliberately constituted domain organized around an intended capability**. It draws upon existing subjects and knowledge where relevant, develops their necessary connections, and creates the additional objects and constructive arrangements that the capability requires.

Its scope may cut across inherited boundaries. What makes it a domain is its connected organization of subjects, meanings and work; what makes it deliberately capability-oriented is the role of the intended accomplishment in shaping that organization. The latter is a specification of the former, not a replacement definition for every use of *domain*.

### 4.1 The role of purpose

The *telos*, or intended purpose, states what is to be made possible. It helps determine the required capability, which in turn exposes relevant domains, depths and connecting obligations. This reasoning can be revised as the work reveals missing knowledge, incompatible requirements or alternative adequate organizations.

Purpose does not uniquely select the means. Two different constitutions may support the intended work, while an initially plausible one may fail. The account must explain why its selected subjects and relationships are adequate rather than derive their correctness merely from wanting the result.

### 4.2 What arbitrary means in this setting

*Arbitrarily specified* means that the intended domain or capability need not come from a predetermined catalogue of disciplines, products or prebuilt configurations. It can select, combine and reorganize contributions in a new way, including defining new intermediate subjects and possibilities of work.

It does not make adopted facts arbitrary, permit incompatible commitments or establish every imagined capability as realizable. Once an intended scope is proposed, its subjects, laws, dependencies and realizing powers have to be adequately accounted for.

The freedom concerns what can be proposed for constitution. The resulting organization must answer to its own meanings, grounds and constructive obligations. This distinction gives the word *arbitrary* its intended force without using it to erase rigor.

### 4.3 One occurrence and a standing capability

Designing one arrangement and establishing a capability to design an admitted class of arrangements are different tasks. A standing capability needs an account of the variation among future requirements, not only the relations exercised by one successful example.

General formation and composition laws can cover cases not individually anticipated. The definition must explain which structures and work belong to that class; its operative realization must establish the powers claimed over it. This stronger ambition is central when the domain is developed for an abstographic capability.

### 4.4 Reconstruction and creation develop together

Deliberate constitution can preserve an inherited object, give an existing distinction a more explicit form, introduce a joint subject or develop a new transformation. These are different kinds of contribution, and one programme can require all of them.

Possible objects and possible work are developed reciprocally. A task can reveal a needed comparison basis or intermediate; an object can enable tasks whose results and means then require definition. The domain is neither merely extracted from sources nor freely invented without regard to its subjects and purpose.

## 5. What a formal domain definition is

**A formal domain definition is an explicit, coherently connected account of a domain's subjects, meanings, relationships and admitted forms of inquiry or activity, giving the exact constitutive structure needed to determine their identity, interpretation, use and consequences at the declared scope.** It explains what belongs to the domain, how its constituents relate and which laws and premises govern the constructions and claims made through them.

This can concern an inherited domain, a reconstructed integration of domains or a deliberately designed domain. Those origins change the work of definition and its grounds; they do not make formal definition exclusive to the last case.

For an existing subject, the definition must distinguish making an account explicit from changing what the account concerns. For a designed formal object, definition can constitute the object itself. For a productive capability, the account must additionally connect possible tasks, products, constructive means and their intended effects. The exact content follows the scope being claimed, not a single template imposed on all domains.

### 5.1 Four different questions

| Question | What is being established |
| --- | --- |
| What is the domain? | Its subjects, scope and internal organization, including its relation to wider fields and other domains. |
| How did this organization arise, or how is it being constituted? | What is inherited, reconstructed, integrated or deliberately designed. |
| What has been formally defined? | Which meanings and relationships have an adequate explicit, exact account, and which remain incomplete. |
| What can actually be done through it? | Which constructive means and realizing arrangements support which accomplishments under which premises. |

These questions are connected but not equivalent. A domain can exist before an adequate formal definition of it. A designed domain can still be underdefined. A formal definition can be substantial without supplying an operative apparatus. Existing practice can also perform work for which its formal account remains incomplete.

### 5.2 Explanation and exact structure

The definition needs explanatory and formal adequacy together. Explanation makes the subjects, distinctions and relationships intelligible. Formal expression gives consequential relationships the exact structure required for reasoning, composition and preservation. The account must establish that these presentations concern the same commitments.

This is more than moving descriptions into a technical notation. A named relation such as *depends upon* still needs its relevant subjects, roles, scope, premises and consequences. An identifier does not alone specify identity through change. Conversely, a detailed formal object can be inadequate if its interpretation omits a distinction that the domain needs.

Different parts may require different instruments. A family of values, a partial relation, a structured history and a cross-world correspondence need not have the same form. Their joint account must explain how the contributions connect; one syntax or one uniform mathematical structure is not presumed.

### 5.3 Formal definition and productive operability

Where productive work is claimed, its tasks and transformations belong in the definition with their necessary inputs, intermediate objects and results. A catalogue of nouns plus a separate list of activities is insufficient if their relationships remain unstated.

Actual realization is a further responsibility that informs this constitutive work throughout. An attempted operation can reveal a missing premise; a new formal distinction can require a different realizing mechanism. Definition and realization are distinct achievements without being isolated, once-only phases.

The deeper account of constituting and exercising an abstographic capability belongs to Abstography. The present account supplies the domain-definition depth needed to understand that relationship, without making every field or domain synonymous with an Abstographic machine.

## 6. What is inherited, constituted and realized

An adequate definition must identify the different origins and grounds of its constituents. An existing subject, a received account of it, a reconstructed relationship and a newly designed object are not the same kind of contribution. They can participate in one domain without being given the same justification.

| Contribution to a domain | What the definition does | What warrants its use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account of an existing subject | Selects and expresses the distinctions needed to represent it at the declared scope. | Adequacy of that account to the subject and intended use. |
| Adopted formal structure | Gives an existing mathematical or logical construction a specified domain interpretation. | Its formal properties and the suitability of that interpretation. |
| Reconstructed connection | Makes explicit a relationship dispersed across sources or practice. | Grounds for the reconstruction, including the assumptions it introduces. |
| Designed constituent | Defines a new object, relation or arrangement required by the intended work. | Its coherent constitution and its adequacy for the stated constructive role. |
| Operative realization | Gives a defined responsibility a means of actual performance. | Correspondence between the defined responsibility and the realizing behavior. |

For a deliberately designed domain, responsibility includes what is represented, what is newly formed, how those parts relate and what claims are being made about them. For an inherited domain, a new definition can make its organization more explicit while also proposing revisions. Description and revision must be distinguished: discovering that an inherited account is inadequate is not permission to present a replacement as though it had always been the original meaning.

The distinction also prevents a different error. Newly required objects need not be extracted from a pre-existing source. A comparison basis, a family of supporting configurations or a joint subject spanning worlds can have a legitimate place because the intended work requires its construction.

## 7. The connected dimensions of a definition

The dimensions below concern the organization of the domain being defined. Their required depth depends on its subjects and the inquiry, practice or productive responsibility claimed for the account. They are not mandatory software components or an assumption that every domain must become a machine.

| Dimension | Definitional responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| Ontological | What kinds of subject exist, how they are individuated and what constitutes a composite. |
| Semantic | What objects and relations mean, in which contexts, and how that meaning bears on their use. |
| Structural and logical | Which formations and combinations are admitted, which laws apply and which combinations are excluded. |
| Activity and construction | Which inquiries, inferences, comparisons, transformations or task families belong to the account; where productive capability is claimed, which products and constructive means support it. |
| Interfacial | How constituents become available for internal, external or cross-world use, with what premises and guarantees. |
| Situational and developmental | Which states, changes and configurations matter, and how they affect possible work or existing results. |
| Realizational | Where a representation, instrument or operative apparatus is included, how it realizes the claimed structure or activity and how its effects correspond to the account. |
| Epistemic | Which claims are definitions, consequences, assumptions, observations or unresolved questions where that difference affects use. |

The most consequential structures often occur at their intersections. The same object's identity can matter to a transformation, the interpretation of a result and the propagation of a change. Defining these independently without their connecting laws can leave the overall construction underdefined.

## 8. Objects, composites and interfaces

An adequate definition of an object requires more than a label. Depending on its nature, the account can require identity, type, constituents, relationships, scope, admissible states and laws of participation in further inquiry or construction. Which of these describe an existing subject and which constitute a designed object must remain clear.

A composite requires the relationships that make its constituents parts of this object. A subsequent inquiry or construction may need one constituent, an indexed family of constituents, their supports or the entire connected structure. An interface specifies the relation of access or use, its premises and what it preserves. This is a mathematical or conceptual responsibility before it is any particular software interface.

Consider a construction selecting components within assemblies. It can constitute a family of qualifying configurations, a family of selected assembly–component units, and the supporting configurations belonging to each unit. Counting the supports, counting those units and counting distinct components are different operations. One component may participate in several assemblies; a unique assembly accessor on its global identity would then require justification rather than follow from its name.

The point is general: **the objects a construction generates, the relations connecting them and the interfaces through which they can be used all belong to its definition.** A final count or truth value may be one result without exhausting the structure required for further work.

Availability belongs to this account where it matters. A defined empty family, an unavailable measurement and a defined false statement can imply different continuations. Which distinctions a particular domain needs, and how they propagate through its interfaces, must be established from the actual construction rather than imposed through one universal status vocabulary.

## 9. Worlds, contexts and shared identity

A formal account can organize a domain through several differently constituted worlds or contexts. A world here supplies an explicit organization in which particular objects, relations and interpretations have their place. The domain names the connected subject being accounted for; its worlds distinguish formal organizations within that account. A source field need not correspond to exactly one world, and a world may integrate contributions from several fields.

One world can emphasize structural relations, another functional organization, and another a substrate's objects and operations. Their contributions need not be reducible to one identical schema. The developed account of formal universes and worlds in [Abstography, §5](<../Abstography/01-ABSTOGRAPHY-WORKING-DEFINITION.md>) supplies deeper context for that productive setting. The present distinction does not require every domain definition to adopt one world architecture.

The same formal object can participate in more than one world, with different local relationships. Its shared identity does not require copying every local property into every participation. Conversely, similar names do not establish shared identity between distinct objects.

The definition must explain what is common and what remains local. Depending on the intended organization, this can concern shared populations, common properties, context-dependent relations or explicit correspondences between distinct subjects. Joint use requires those relationships to be available with the meaning the construction needs.

Compatibility of separate local accounts does not automatically establish a coherent whole. The account must state how the relevant contributions connect and which assembly or preservation properties hold. Coverings, sheaves or other mathematical structures may be useful where their actual laws fit; their names are not substitutes for defining the relationship.

An inquiry, comparison or constructive operation can concern configurations spanning several worlds, including constituents generated by other constructions. Such use can be genuinely joint rather than a sequence of isolated local questions. In an abstographic productive account, Processes and Conditions can have this joint scope; their fuller roles are developed in [Abstography, §§7–10](<../Abstography/01-ABSTOGRAPHY-WORKING-DEFINITION.md>). These are important possibilities, not an instruction to put the same operating machinery into every formal domain definition.

## 10. Inquiry, activity and possible constructive work

The internal organization of a domain includes how its subjects can be investigated, related and worked upon. A comparison needs something to compare and a meaningful basis; an inference needs interpretable premises and a consequence relation; a transformation needs a subject and an account of what changes or is preserved. These relationships matter before deciding whether an automated apparatus will exercise them.

In a productive domain, possible tasks develop with possible objects. A task needs meaningful subjects and circumstances; its product needs a constitution and an intended use. A proposed object can enable new work, while a task can expose a previously missing subject or intermediate structure.

The account must distinguish:

- The accomplishment required by a task.
- The admitted situations in which it is meaningful.
- The means or family of routes through which it can be performed.
- The object or change that counts as its result.
- The preservation obligations and permissible effects of its performance.

Where the account claims constructive powers, a constructor's task repertoire, its configuration and a particular occurrence of its work are different matters. A fixed program can support different constructive courses as its input composition changes. The standing definition concerns the meanings and powers through which that variation is possible, not an enumeration of every future occurrence.

Different kinds of constructor can supply different powers. The domain must identify those powers where they are required, along with their inputs, effects and interfaces. Naming a technology does not fill in a missing transformation account.

This is part of the relationship to Abstography. Its established account treats domain constitution and possible constructive activity together. That productive specialisation must neither replace the general account of fields and domains nor be portrayed as an unrelated activation mechanism attached after all domain meanings are complete. Its tasks, constructor kinds and interfaces have their deeper explanation in [Abstography, §§6–7](<../Abstography/01-ABSTOGRAPHY-WORKING-DEFINITION.md>).

## 11. Possibility space, actuality and change

The definition of admitted kinds and formations is distinct from a current population or one encountered case. A formal domain account can specify what constitutes an object, relationship or question without enumerating all its instances. It can also reconstruct the constitution of instances that already exist. Definition before future population is a useful possibility, not a claim that every domain begins without actuality.

General formation laws can admit infinitely many constructions, including arbitrarily large permitted finite structures. The relevant boundary concerns the kinds of objects and work, their composition laws and, where realization is claimed, its premises. Whether a particular domain has an infinite possibility space follows from its account; it is not established simply by calling it a domain. The bounded, infinite productive commitment of Abstography is a more specific requirement, developed there in §§3–4.

An infinite possibility space does not eliminate resource requirements or establish that every imagined task is attainable.

When new objects appear, they can create new subjects for existing task families. An arrangement may introduce comparison questions or dependencies that did not previously have instances. That can exercise a standing capability rather than extend its defining laws.

Change also raises identity and dependency questions. Which transformations preserve an object? Which create a successor or a different object? When does a result remain applicable after its inputs change? Which consequences reach another world or consumer? These questions need their own domain-relative laws.

The existence of a result, the state from which it was constructed and the conditions under which it can now be used are connected but distinct. Their representation should follow the constructive responsibility, not automatically collapse into a timestamp or one undifferentiated status.

## 12. Domain mapping and domain definition

*Domain mapping*, in the developing methodological sense used here, names the work of identifying, reconstructing and, where required, developing the relationships through which a domain can receive an adequate account. A *domain definition* names the resulting connected account at a particular stage. Mapping an inherited domain and constituting a new capability-oriented domain share some responsibilities but differ in what is being described, selected or created.

The work can include reconstruction, invention, formalization, criticism and revision. It must establish which is taking place. It does not treat existing knowledge as unorganized material merely because its explicit account is incomplete, nor presume that every relationship required by a new integration already exists in a source.

For a capability-oriented undertaking, several directions inform one another:

1. An intended capability reveals subjects and differences that matter.
2. Proposed tasks reveal inputs, intermediate achievements and constructive powers they require.
3. Proposed products reveal the constituents and interfaces their later use needs.
4. Sources and existing means reveal knowledge that can be adopted, limits that must be respected and gaps that remain.
5. Composition and realization expose relationships absent from the initial plan.
6. Those findings revise the relevant definitions and their actual dependents.

These are directions of reasoning rather than compulsory phases. A missing distinction need not always be repaired by adding a new object; an existing relation, interface or interpretation may need correction instead. The chosen repair should explain what it makes possible and why its added commitments are needed.

Mapping also needs a disciplined relationship to its scope and purpose. In an inherited-domain account, it must not claim to have defined the whole while silently covering only the available fragments. In a capability-oriented account, the intended accomplishment may require legitimate revision, but a failed attempt cannot silently redefine it as whatever happened to succeed. Changed scope and its consequences should be explicit.

This document establishes the nature of that work. Its detailed methodology, instruments and evaluation procedures remain to be developed separately or in later sections as needed.

## 13. Adequacy has several meanings

The questions below assess different claims. Not every domain definition promises an operative machine or industrial capability. The account must state which achievements it undertakes and what grounds are appropriate to them.

| Question | What must be established |
| --- | --- |
| Is the account intelligible? | Its objects, meanings and relationships can be understood without guessing consequential missing commitments. |
| Is it formally coherent? | The relevant definitions, laws and combinations have the claimed consistency or other appropriate formal properties. |
| Is it adequate to the intended domain? | It preserves the distinctions and relationships required by the subject and the scope of inquiry or activity being claimed. |
| Are its admitted uses properly defined? | Interpretation, inference, comparison or construction has the required premises, subjects and interfaces. |
| Where constructive capability is claimed, is it supported? | Required inputs, intermediate objects and transformations have adequate formation and use accounts connected to suitable means. |
| Where operative realization is claimed, does it exist? | Actual means perform the required activity and preserve the defined relationships. |
| Is its claimed reach established? | The evidence and arguments support the scope asserted, rather than only selected favorable cases. |

These achievements interact without collapsing into one certification. A checked formal expression can have the wrong interpretation. A successful operation can leave the general scope unestablished. A good conceptual definition can still lack a suitable realizing apparatus.

For an abstographic capability, the established definition supplies the stronger ambition of attaining admitted requirements throughout its bounded possibility space. Merely representing those requirements is insufficient. The present document must explain its contribution to that ambition without claiming that a domain definition alone proves attainment.

## 14. Completeness relative to an intended scope

Completeness should concern the definitional responsibility being claimed. An account of an inherited domain, an integration for a particular inquiry and a constituted productive capability need not owe the same whole. Each must make its boundary and obligations intelligible. Completeness is neither the accumulation of everything that can be said about a field nor a convenient cutoff at what current tools can express.

A consequential gap exists when the claimed account or activity requires a distinction, constituent, relationship or power that has not been adequately supplied. That includes cases where separate parts are each described but their necessary combination has no defined meaning. A missing interpretation can be such a gap even when no productive apparatus is being proposed.

For example, a product may depend on an intermediate structure whose formation has never been established; a consumer may require an identity that an earlier passage discards; or a task may presuppose its own future result as an initial input. These are gaps in the construction, not just missing documentation.

A definition can be valuable while incomplete. The claim should identify what is established, what remains open and how the open matter affects intended work. Formalization can make specified relationships explicit while leaving substantive interpretation, judgment or inquiry still required. Those remaining responsibilities should be identifiable; their existence does not make the explicit work worthless. Future discoveries can legitimately deepen a domain or change its scope without erasing the reason its account was revised.

The precise notion of completeness appropriate to different kinds of domain is itself a subject for further definition. No universal decision procedure for domain adequacy is asserted here.

## 15. Why making domain definition explicit matters

Practitioners often supply relationships that the materials they use leave unstated. They determine the relevant occurrence, select an interpretation, recognize a missing premise and connect one result to another responsibility. This can be sophisticated, justified work.

Making the relevant constitution explicit can preserve more of that work, expose disagreements, support inquiry, teaching and reuse, and reduce the need to reconstruct the same relationships for every occurrence. In productive settings, it can also reveal that the desired capability requires newly created structures rather than merely more detailed documentation of current practice.

The aim is to make this constitutive activity increasingly understandable and practicable across domains. Appropriate explanation, mathematical instruments and constructive means can help. Their purpose is to support the domain's required depth, not force its nature into the representation most convenient to an early implementation.

Human judgment, situated knowledge or authority may be necessary constituents in some domains. They should receive exact places where needed. They are not a reason to make every possible formal construction pass through an identical assessment-and-approval architecture.

## 18. Development discipline and source status

Improve definitions and explanations together. A changed concept must be reconciled with its dependents, examples and claims of adequacy. Preserve distinctions that perform necessary work; remove duplicate or superseded formulations rather than accumulating them.

The two old sketches are developmental sources, not completed governing definitions:

- [Earlier industrial/domain essay](<C:/Users/Graphics/Desktop/Episteme Messaging/Old Sketches for Essays/Domain Industrialization/Public Essay Rational Industrial Revolution Clean.md>), particularly its Human Compilers section, Contemporary Theoretical Constitution of Domains section and §1.3, supplies the progression from existing knowledge and relative field/domain organization to productive integration and deliberate capability constitution. It also establishes the contrast between local formal knowledge and an explicit connected domain account.
- [Earlier Studio outline](<C:/Users/Graphics/Desktop/Episteme Messaging/Old Sketches for Essays/Outlining Episteme/EPISTEME-AN-OUTLINE THE PROPER ONE.md>) raises questions about reconstructed knowledge, constructive means and industrial use.

Being a sketch does not make its conceptual structure disposable. Retain and deepen the relationships that explain the subject; assess its particular formulations and unsupported generalizations separately. The present work develops that understanding into a coherent definition rather than merely polishing its prose or replacing its foundations with a later specialized meaning.
