# Psychology as a domain: two connected semantic reconstructions

Date: 9 October 2026. Supporting investigation only; the clean working definition was read but not edited. The primary investigator must assess these findings and personally read decisive passages before integration.

## Question, scope and evidence status

How does meaningful psychological knowledge about relevance and intrinsic motivation become a situated judgment or instructional construction, and what would need deeper constitution to make its use operable?

The old working definition §§1–5 already distinguishes concepts, interpretations, inquiry, construction and realization. Its §3.2 lists contributions, and §15.2 lists what realization must preserve. The unresolved explanation is how a particular meaningful expression acquires a consequential subject and justified use. These cases supply that explanation without claiming a complete formal psychology or an exact account of mental mechanisms.

Evidence codes used below:

- **Source:** a distinction or example directly present in the passages read. A book's report of research remains that book's report; the underlying experiments were not independently inspected.
- **Reconstruction:** an explanation of what the source's situated use requires. This is reasoned interpretation of the source, not its verbatim theory.
- **Proposal:** a candidate constitution for a bounded domain of research interpretation or instructional design. Its rules can constitute representations and procedures; they do not establish empirical psychological laws.
- **Constructed challenge:** a hypothetical case exposing different consequences. It is not a reported experimental finding.

This uses psychology in its own domain role: its constructs, explanations, measurement interpretations, empirical methods and applied design judgments are the subjects under examination. It does not infer a general cognitive architecture from these passages.

## Investigation 1: what it means to make learning relevant

### What Keller actually supplies

**Source.** At lines 2151–2167, Keller identifies relevance with people's feelings or perceptions of attraction toward outcomes, ideas or people in relation to their goals, motives and values. He explicitly rejects immediate practical usefulness as an exhaustive account. His torque/acceleration comparison distinguishes an auto-repair course's immediate application from a theoretical-physics course's different situation; the dinosaur example demonstrates personally valued inquiry without immediate utility. At 2171–2177, valuation can concern an outcome as an end or its perceived contribution to further outcomes, or both.

The account does more than supply synonyms. It makes distinctions through contrasts that a reader can recognize: required attendance versus chosen pursuit; learning valued now versus useful later; obtaining a car as pleasurable versus using it for other ends. These examples teach how an apparently identical object can enter different relations to a person. The account also distinguishes a connection perceived by the learner from an instructor's declaration that something will be useful. In the forethought, 2125, the teacher must understand the content's importance and demonstrate utility convincingly where utility is asserted.

**Source.** Keller then supplies an internal complication. At 2408–2426, material can be interesting and topically connected yet irrelevant to the main message or learning objective. Pictures or anecdotes about hurricane destruction concern hurricanes but need not explain hurricane formation. Illustrations of developing cloud formations bear differently on that explanatory purpose. Keller reports studies in which interesting additions reduced recall of important information; he also reports differing proposed explanations of this effect. This is a report about those studies and materials, not a universal prohibition on interesting examples.

His source therefore contains at least two consequential meanings: personal perceived value, and contribution to the message or instructional objective. They can coincide, differ or interfere. A design that joins them must preserve both.

### The chain from meaningful language to actual application

Consider choosing illustrations for a lesson whose adopted objective is to explain hurricane formation. This is a reconstruction of the source's illustrated contrast, extended into a bounded design task.

1. **Understand the psychological expression.** Reading “personal interests” requires more than recognizing a topic label. A person may seek an activity because of curiosity, pleasure, a desired occupation, an immediate worry or another valued outcome. The reader must understand an interest as a person–subject relation, not as a property inherent in the image. Keller's person–stimulus discussion at 2404 and his examples supply this distinction.
2. **Identify the people and the occurrence.** The designer must establish which learners, which lesson and which time the claim concerns. A teacher's prior observation of a class, an individual statement and an estimate about a subgroup support different scopes. A subgroup description does not become a finding about every member. Keller's audience worksheet and accompanying qualifications, 4143–4157, already organize this responsibility partly.
3. **Identify what the learner values.** “I am interested in storms” might mean wanting to understand weather mechanisms, being concerned about coastal safety, enjoying dramatic survival stories, or wanting a maritime career. Reading those sentences meaningfully requires learned concepts of explanation, safety, narrative excitement and occupational purpose. The designer must inquire or retain alternatives where the intended relation is not known. The book does not contain a lookup rule turning the word “storms” into one motive.
4. **Understand the instructional objective.** “Explain formation” requires understanding a process and the relationships that constitute an explanation of it. Recognizing a damaged sailboat as an effect of a hurricane does not make it a representation of that formation process. The designer needs subject knowledge to distinguish causes, stages, conditions and consequences. This is an epistemic contribution from the weather domain inside a motivational-design task.
5. **Inspect the candidate's actual content.** The cloud diagram must be read through its arrows, sequence, labels or depicted relationships. The sailboat photograph must be recognized as depicting damage and its possible context. A file tag “hurricane” supplies neither interpretation. A diagram can also be inaccurate or too difficult; identifying its explanatory role does not settle its factual or pedagogical adequacy.
6. **Construct a relationship between learner purpose and lesson work.** If learners want to understand storm formation, a suitable process illustration can directly join their desired understanding to the instructional objective. If they care about safe sailing, the designer can form a bridge: understanding specified formation conditions may inform a later topic concerning storm development and safety. That bridge requires a defensible account of what understanding contributes; it cannot silently turn a formation lesson into a complete safety course.
7. **Exclude an invalid shortcut.** Topic overlap and attention capture do not license the conclusion that a candidate contributes to explaining formation. The hurricane counterexample teaches this exclusion. Conversely, a picture of damage might legitimately introduce the importance of the topic without itself teaching formation. The role of the candidate within the lesson matters.
8. **Form an intermediate design.** The useful result is not the judgment “this is relevant.” It is an arrangement: perhaps briefly introduce a valued concern, state the formation question, then use the diagram and questions that direct attention to specified explanatory relationships. An introduction, an explanation and an assessment perform different functions even when they concern the same storm.
9. **Adopt and evaluate.** The designer selects an arrangement on stated grounds. That creates the lesson plan and its intended role assignments. It does not create the learners' actual perceptions, guarantee learning, or prove that this arrangement caused a later response. Reports, classroom work and appropriately designed evaluation supply further evidence.

These are functional dependencies, not a proposed chronological sequence inside a person's mind. The learned competence contributes determinate content at each dependency: identifying effects versus formation, a valued end versus a means, a person-specific interest versus a general topic, and an introductory role versus an explanatory role. A teacher, learner discussion, accepted weather model, worksheet and existing instructional practice can supply different parts. It would be false to assign them all to an unnamed mental interpreter.

### Different interpretations with the same coarse data

**Constructed challenge.** Suppose a learner says “I like storms,” selects dramatic storm photographs, spends ten minutes with the lesson and rates it highly. The same coarse record admits materially different interpretations:

| Interpretation | Relationship actually represented | Consequence for design or evaluation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The learner seeks a causal explanation | Attraction concerns understanding formation | Explanatory diagrams and mechanism questions can pursue the valued end; performance still needs assessment. |
| The learner enjoys survival spectacle | Attraction concerns dramatic events or consequences | Extra destruction photographs may support attention but fail the adopted explanatory objective; a bridge must be constructed and evaluated. |
| The learner wants an examination result | The lesson is perceived as a means to an external outcome | A high rating or long participation does not establish interest in the formation process itself. |
| The learner is worried about family safety | Attraction or concern concerns preventing harm | The lesson's relation to that concern must be explained accurately; neither duration nor interest tags establish that it addresses the practical concern. |

These are possible interpretations of a hypothetical record, not psychological diagnoses. The designer cannot choose among them merely by computing the greatest topic similarity. Nor does one interpretation force one tactic independently of knowledge, circumstances and the accepted educational purpose.

### Candidate constitution: a reasoned connection rather than a relevance score

**Proposal.** Constitute a limited domain of lesson-design candidates and their grounds. The task is to construct an accountable connection between learner-valued purposes and specified instructional work; it is not to certify a learner's complete motivation.

The necessary distinctions have content and consequences:

- A **learner's expressed valuation** is an assertion attributed to a person, about an identified subject or outcome, at a context and time. It retains its wording and grounds. It is not identical to a disposition possessed for all contexts. A statement about a subgroup cannot automatically instantiate a statement about an individual.
- An **instructional objective** is an adopted description of the understanding or performance the lesson is intended to enable. It is not an observation that the learner desires that outcome, and not a claim that the learner has achieved it.
- A **candidate's instructional role** identifies what it supplies in the arrangement: introducing a question, representing a process, supplying an analogy, exercising a distinction, or assessing performance. An effect photograph can fill an introductory role without being admitted as evidence that it explains formation.
- A **perceived means–end connection** is an attributed belief about how one outcome contributes to another. Its believer, connected outcomes and context remain explicit. It is distinct from an established causal or practical dependency. Discovering that a learner believes a prerequisite helps a career does not establish that prerequisite's actual efficacy.
- A **design connection** contains a reasoned path, not simply an edge: the learner-valued end; the instructional achievement; the candidate's role in making that achievement available; the premises supporting the connection; and any further instruction or external conditions needed. If one step changes its subject or purpose, a bridge must explain and justify that change.

For the formation example, a candidate connection can state: learners have expressed a desire to understand how storms develop; the objective is to explain specified formation relationships; this diagram represents those relationships; these questions require learners to use them. These components establish a proposed alignment. They do not establish that the diagram is scientifically true, that the expression exhausts the learner's reasons, or that the learner will experience the alignment.

A bounded generative construction can then be exact in several respects. Permit a plan to compose an introductory component, explanatory components and assessment components only when each has a declared target and role. An explanatory component must identify the relationships it presents and the accepted domain account to which it refers. An assessment must identify the target relationship it asks the learner to use. Where a component is intended to bridge different purposes, require a stated intermediate achievement and premises linking the purposes. A purely topically associated component cannot discharge the explanatory obligation by itself. These are formation and checking rules for accountable plans; they are not psychological laws guaranteeing an effect.

Composition has real consequences. A photograph followed by a diagram is not adequate just because each has an admitted role. The connection between them must direct the learner from consequences to the formation question; the time, prerequisites and intended assessment must remain compatible with that transition. Adding an unrelated dramatic sequence may change that arrangement even though its image tags stay identical. The definition must express the relevant connection, while evaluation investigates how this particular presentation works.

The generative capability is stronger than storing one selected image: it admits many plans over the same family of roles, targets and bridge obligations, preserves why their components enter, and detects a candidate that substitutes topic overlap for explanation. It does not claim that every admitted plan succeeds.

### Limits and realization implications

**Source.** Keller already offers distributed means: a future wheel has learners construct proposed benefit chains (2615), a facilitated exercise elicits and compares problem descriptions (2643–2651), and the rural-town/city example creates a bridge from familiar experience to a new subject (2739–2745). These are existing constructive methods. The future wheel forms possible benefits perceived by learners; it does not prove all its paths. The town example uses familiar food-supply relationships, exposes where existing knowledge stops, and creates questions that the new material can answer. Its semantic action is richer than simply matching a familiar topic.

**Proposal.** A realizing environment could instantiate the plan roles, collect learner statements, keep alternative interpretations, require a justified bridge, retain dependency grounds and connect assessments to objectives. It must have means for interpreting content or obtaining that interpretation from a declared contributor. “An expert provides the interpretation” needs a defined deliverable: which relationships the material represents, its intended role, supporting grounds and limits. It cannot stand for unrestricted invisible judgment.

What remains empirical includes actual learner values, whether the stated connection is perceived, the scientific truth of imported content, and the motivational and learning effects of the presentation. What can be constituted includes the adopted objective, candidate-plan structure, required connection grounds, role assignments, assessment references and revision procedure. Making those exact supports inquiry; it does not legislate psychological findings.

## Investigation 2: what an operational indicator permits one to infer

### What Deci and Ryan actually supply

**Source.** Their psychological definition at 764 and surrounding explanation uses competence and self-determination, activity-directed interest, effectance and autonomy. At 786–788 they connect intrinsic motivation to experiences of interest and enjoyment and distinguish pressure and tension. Their account is a substantive theoretical interpretation, not a definition of intrinsic motivation as a count of actions.

At 790–800 they explicitly distinguish the psychological definition from operational definitions used in research. Activity in the absence of reward contingency or control is an indicator; it is not perfectly correlated with the psychological definition. They discuss pressure and ego involvement as reasons to investigate another motivational dynamic despite apparent absence of external reward. They also identify creativity, flexibility and spontaneity as possible indicators, and questionnaires as another route. Their enjoyment example asks whether the person enjoyed doing the activity or receiving the reward. The referent changes the evidential relevance.

At 804 and 838–850 the source distinguishes self-determined extrinsic action from intrinsic action, and distinguishes self-determination from control. A person can choose an instrumental action, or choose to delegate control. Thus externally unrewarded action and choice do not erase the distinction between valuing an activity for itself and valuing an outcome it helps achieve.

### The chain from observation to warranted use

Consider interpreting a research record containing activity duration, an enjoyment answer and information about rewards. This reconstructs the source's central warning, not a clinical assessment.

1. **Identify the construct question.** The question concerns a person's motivation for a specified activity in a specified episode, under the adopted theoretical interpretation. It is not merely whether the person moved, performed well or liked something.
2. **Identify the activity.** “Doing puzzles” must distinguish the experimental activity, the later free-choice opportunity and the reward event. If the record concerns the whole paid experience, the investigator cannot silently bind its enjoyment rating to puzzle solving alone. The participant's response may itself need clarification.
3. **Understand the indicator procedure.** “Free choice” is meaningful through more than physical absence of the experimenter. Relevant context includes alternatives, communicated conditions, awareness of observation, expected consequences and prior contingencies. The source's procedure at 932–942 explicitly constructs an opportunity with other activities, no apparent reason to work for an extrinsic consequence and observation precautions. A duration value inherits that procedural context.
4. **Separate the observed result from its interpretation.** The timer or observer produces a record of target-activity engagement. A questionnaire produces an answer to particular wording. These are evidence-bearing occurrences; neither is identical to the unobserved psychological process.
5. **Bind the answer's meaningful target.** “Very enjoyable” can concern manipulating the puzzle, receiving payment, doing well relative to another participant, or the whole experience. Recognizing those alternatives requires concepts of action, consequence, evaluation and self-worth. The numerical magnitude alone lacks the relation the psychological inference needs.
6. **Consider competing explanations.** Continued activity without an immediate external reward may express activity-directed interest, self-imposed pressure, a self-chosen instrumental goal or an expectation not captured by the record. Pressure and tension bear on the interpretation but do not by themselves identify a unique cause. The source's recommendation is to investigate more deeply, not to diagnose a complete motivational process from one sign.
7. **Form a qualified inference.** Activity-directed enjoyment and engagement under appropriately characterized choice conditions can provide support for the construct in the adopted account. An ambiguous enjoyment answer does not provide that same support. The source supplies no universal numerical threshold or complete calculus determining exactly how much support these observations give.
8. **Distinguish inference from causal attribution.** Even a supported interpretation of motivation in an episode does not establish that a particular reward or teaching tactic caused it. The experiments introduce comparisons and procedural controls for a different inferential task. A classroom observation or lone questionnaire answer does not acquire those grounds by using the same construct name.
9. **Form the usable result.** The result should identify the activity and episode, evidence, interpretation, remaining alternatives and limits of permitted use. A further design decision can use that result on explicitly adopted grounds. It cannot retrospectively strengthen the evidence merely because a decision is needed.

The researcher supplies more than caution. The contribution includes discriminating the object of enjoyment, distinguishing a behavioral procedure from a construct, relating tension to alternative regulation within the adopted account, and recognizing which experimental premises are needed for a group comparison. Instruments already supply observation and timing; protocols already shape a choice opportunity; the research community supplies constructs and methods. Their connections still have to be established in the case.

### Equal coarse records, incompatible conclusions

**Constructed challenge.** Three records all contain ten minutes of optional puzzle engagement, no recorded payment and an enjoyment response of 5/5:

| Further interpretation or evidence | What the coarse record conceals | Permissible consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The participant enjoyed manipulating and solving the puzzle | Enjoyment concerns the target activity | It can support an intrinsic-motivation interpretation within the adopted account and context; it does not guarantee it. |
| The participant enjoyed demonstrating superiority and felt pressured to avoid failure | The episode includes evaluative self-pressure | Persistence is not sufficient to classify the process as simply intrinsic; inquiry into the regulation remains needed. |
| The participant wants to improve for a freely adopted future qualification and enjoys making progress | The activity serves a valued further end | The record may support self-determined instrumental action; choice alone does not establish intrinsic motivation for the activity. |

A separate reward variant is directly source-grounded: after a paid task, a high enjoyment rating can concern activity pleasure or getting the reward (800). The same coarse value then has different evidential relevance even before competing causal models are considered.

These alternatives are not automatically mutually exclusive. An episode may combine activity pleasure with another reason. A representation requiring every person to have exactly one motivational label would introduce a claim the passages do not warrant.

### Candidate constitution: evidence whose interpretation has a defined subject

**Proposal.** A bounded research-interpretation domain needs the following distinctions because each blocks a particular invalid inference:

| Distinction | Required content and relationship | Error prevented |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Activity / episode / opportunity / consequence | The activity being considered; its occurrences; the offered alternatives and communicated conditions; subsequent or contingent outcomes | Treating all enjoyment or action within an experiment as directed at the same subject |
| Observation / report / interpretation | Who recorded what by which procedure; what the participant answered; how the investigator relates the record to a construct | Replacing reported or inferred states with directly observed facts |
| Construct / operational measure | The theoretical meaning; the procedure and evidence-bearing result; the grounds and limits of its use as an indicator | Defining the construct as whatever a timer or questionnaire returns |
| Absence of recorded contingency / established procedural absence | The actual scope of the recording or assurance, including what consequences and perceptions were considered | Turning missing data into proof that no extrinsic reason exists |
| Activity-directed enjoyment / consequence-directed enjoyment / unresolved referent | The subject to which the answer is bound and the basis of that binding | Treating equal scores as interchangeable evidence |
| Supported interpretation / competing explanation / causal attribution | A claim about the episode under a model; alternative claims; a claim about an intervention's effect with its required comparison grounds | Turning a plausible construct interpretation into an established intervention effect |
| Adopted decision / warranted finding | The chosen action and the purposes, priorities and evidence on which it is adopted | Treating the need to choose a tactic as evidence that its psychological premises are true |

Merely introducing these object names is insufficient. Candidate use rules must preserve their meaning:

1. An enjoyment result may enter as activity-directed evidence only when its referent has been established with stated grounds. If the wording concerns the whole experience and no further interpretation resolves it, the result retains an unresolved target. It cannot be silently converted to enjoyment of the target activity.
2. Duration enters an inference together with the episode, opportunity and procedure under which it was recorded. A claimed absence of external contingency must identify the covered conditions and its grounds. No rule turns an empty reward field into an assertion of free choice.
3. A construct interpretation carries the theoretical account under which the indicator matters, its evidence and the alternatives not excluded. The source's stated imperfection of operational measures forbids an equivalence between measured duration and intrinsic motivation.
4. A proposed causal attribution requires a separate argument relating conditions, comparison and outcomes. It cannot be derived from a construct label plus temporal succession. The relevant experiment may supply such grounds; a single episode does not.
5. A design decision may proceed under uncertainty, but its result records what was adopted and why. It does not replace an unresolved evidence interpretation with a finding.

These constitute a disciplined family of result formations. They do not constitute a complete evidential calculus. A numerical combination of questionnaire and duration measures would additionally need a justified measurement model and validation appropriate to its use. Those are investigative obligations, not free choices of datatype.

Revision has a precise consequence. If clarification changes an enjoyment result's referent from the puzzle to the payment, the raw answer remains the same record. Its eligibility as activity-directed evidence changes, so interpretations depending on that eligibility must be reconsidered. Observation identity and inferential role are different. Retaining this dependency permits an operative arrangement to revise affected results without rewriting the historical observation.

### Exact knowledge limits and causal-method context

**Source.** In the early three-session puzzle study, 906–918, the source describes baseline and follow-up free-choice measures, a temporary payment manipulation and comparison of changes across experimental and control groups. At 922–930 it presents an alternative emotional-reaction interpretation and methodological concerns about experimenter cues and nonblind observation. Its subsequent one-session procedure uses separated observation and, at 934, explicitly relies on random assignment and adequate cell sizes for interpreting group differences as changes from comparable initial levels.

This context makes the semantic domain richer: “change,” “effect,” “free choice” and “intrinsic motivation” have different grounds. The book's procedural organization already discharges part of that work. Formal constitution should preserve it rather than invent an exact-looking duration rule and lose the experimental question.

What is known here is that these sources explain particular indicators, caveats and methods. This investigation has not established contemporary measurement validity, a unique motivational state for any actual person, or an exhaustive set of possible confounds. Theoretical language such as needs, perceived locus of causality and internal regulation remains a substantive psychological account, with empirical commitments not proven by representing it.

What can be constituted is a question's scope, an indicator's procedure, the meaningful referent required for a given use, the distinction between empirical and adopted claims, an admissible argument structure and revision dependencies. What must be investigated includes whether a person's statement accurately captures experience, which competing explanation fits, and what an intervention caused.

## Connection between the investigations

The first case constructs a proposed lesson relationship. The second interprets evidence about responses to a lesson or activity. They can become a loop, but their joining creates additional responsibilities.

A design may be selected because it is expected to connect instruction to an expressed interest. Participation and a high enjoyment rating after presentation do not, without further work, prove that the learner valued the instructional content, learned its objective, or was intrinsically motivated. They may concern dramatic material, a reward or another outcome. Thus evaluation must bind its evidence to the same constituents whose roles justified the design.

A bounded joined domain could preserve three distinct references: the learner-valued subject used to justify the plan; the instructional relationships the plan intends to teach; and the subject of the later report or performance evidence. Equality of these references must be established, not assumed from the word “lesson.” A result can then show, for example, that the learner enjoyed an introductory image while failing the formation question, or understood formation while participating for a valued qualification. Neither is a contradiction once the subjects and claims are distinguished.

This supports a stronger lesson for formal domain definition. The constitution must make a consequential phrase's referential and inferential dependencies explicit, then preserve them through construction, observation and revision. It must not replace that phrase with an uninterpreted function called relevance or motivation. The source's semantic knowledge makes the dependencies intelligible; a deeper constitution fixes their carriers, permissible uses and obligations at a declared scope.

## Consequences for the clean working definition

Suggested integration is conceptual rather than importing this whole report:

1. Expand the account of existing meaning with source-grounded contrasts. “Relevant” is understood through goals, ends, means, experiences and examples, including a counterexample that distinguishes topically related information from contribution to an explanation. This demonstrates organized semantics without a self-contained operative specification.
2. Replace a generic list of recognition and interpretation with at least one traced use. The decisive reader contributions are identifiable content: binding enjoyment to its object, distinguishing an effect from a formation process, and recognizing a perceived benefit as different from an established dependency.
3. Deepen ontology through one dependency. A report needs a subject, episode, question wording, speaker and grounds of interpretation because changing the referent changes the inference. This is a reason for the objects, not an inventory of possible nodes.
4. Explain constructive possibility with accountable plan formation and qualified research-result formation. Their admission laws can be exact while their empirical achievement remains uncertain. This shows formal constitution enabling inquiry rather than replacing it.
5. Make derived operability concrete. A faithful environment can generate the admitted plan/result structures, enforce the restricted evidence passages, maintain dependency revision and solicit bounded contributions. If it cannot interpret what a diagram explains or what a response concerns, the required contributor must provide that specified result; the claimed automated capability remains correspondingly limited.

The principal contribution is not a claim that psychology is informal. These selected accounts combine substantial semantic articulation, theoretical explanation, experimental procedure, quantitative records and applied construction. Their constitution problems differ within the same domain: identifying a valued end, constructing an instructional bridge, interpreting a measurement and supporting a causal attribution require different structures and grounds.

## Outstanding work

- The proposed formation rules require primary assessment; they are an explanatory construction, not a validated instructional-design method.
- The bounds of a faithful realizing environment must be specified for the selected responsibility. A representation with provenance and typing alone cannot perform content interpretation or empirical observation.
- Any numerical measurement or causal-inference capability would need additional sources, models and validation. These passages establish why that work matters, not its complete solution.
- Integration should retain the distinction between empirical psychological commitments and constitutively adopted procedures. Formal exactness can organize uncertainty without resolving it by definition.
