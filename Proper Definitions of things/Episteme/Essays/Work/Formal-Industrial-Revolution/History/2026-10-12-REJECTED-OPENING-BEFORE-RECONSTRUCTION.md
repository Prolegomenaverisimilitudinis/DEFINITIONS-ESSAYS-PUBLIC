# The Formal Industrial Revolution

*Working definition — the opening foundations, developed 12 October 2026.*

## Introduction

We have acquired an extraordinary technological power in trained language models: programmable probabilistic engines that can perform transformations over our symbolic systems. We can give them explanations, instructions, examples and material to work upon; we can ask them to distinguish, relate, revise and construct. More remarkably, the objects and operations need not all have been individually anticipated. We can introduce a new symbolic arrangement through definitions expressed in languages the engine already handles, and configure work within that arrangement.

The achievement changes what it can mean to program a machine. A substantial part of the interpretation and transformation that would otherwise require task-specific development can be brought into operation through the instructions supplied to an already capable engine. The same underlying capacity can participate in many different undertakings. Its usefulness extends beyond reproducing familiar answers: it can operate upon newly introduced relationships and help construct results whose particular form was not supplied in advance.

This freedom enlarges the field of what we can attempt: the material we can make actionable, the transformations we can configure and the undertakings to which the same underlying power can contribute. But recognizing the magnitude of an achievement is different from understanding its place in the future. A power can be pervasive without being the organizing principle of everything it powers. Its importance can lie in the machinery it enables rather than in its becoming a machine that takes responsibility for everything.

The picture we examine is the opposite: an increasingly capable model imagined as an increasingly accomplished entity, progressing toward a general intelligence that can be placed inside almost any undertaking and assume its work. In that picture, the principal unfinished object is the intelligence itself. Improve it sufficiently, and the difficulties of knowledge, context, judgment and action are expected to become responsibilities it can discharge wherever it is introduced.

Our point of departure is a different possibility. **The probabilistic breakthrough can be an enabling power for a far more formally constituted industrial future. The engine need not become the system; it can make new kinds of system possible.** To understand that proposition, we must first understand what the engine actually supplies, what has been added to it by the image of a general entity, and what productive work continues to require even when its transformative instruments become immensely powerful.

This account therefore begins with the achievement itself. It develops a constructive and technical description of the engine, examines the future imagined around it, and follows the resulting questions toward the relationship between knowledge and industry. That relationship supplies the foundation from which the formal industrial undertaking can subsequently be defined. We begin with what is in our hands, before deciding what kind of future those hands must construct.

## Part I — The power we have acquired

### 1. Programmability over our symbolic systems

The conspicuous interface is conversation. We write, the engine responds, and much of what it produces has the recognizable form of an answer. But conversation is only one way of arranging the encounter. The deeper capability is that instructions can configure transformations over material whose organization and meaning are expressed symbolically.

Our symbolic resources include ordinary language, mathematical notation, programming languages and the specialized expressions through which different fields distinguish their subjects. Their signs acquire significance through relationships: a statement can support another statement, an expression can denote an operation, a name can identify an object, and a rule can determine what may be done with it. Work upon such material can require interpreting these relationships rather than merely replacing one sequence of characters with another.

A trained language model brings learned capacities through which many of these relationships become usable in computation. We can ask it to preserve a distinction while changing an explanation, compare accounts under specified criteria, reorganize an argument, or construct an output whose parts must stand in newly prescribed relationships. The task can be explained through the same symbolic resources in which its material is presented.

That is the significance of calling the engine *programmable* here. A program supplies more than a topic. It configures what work is to be performed: which material counts as input, how it is to be considered, what transformations are called for, and what the resulting construction is meant to accomplish. Instructions, definitions, examples and constraints can cooperate in supplying that organization.

The same engine can receive substantially different programs without being separately trained from the beginning for every one. This does not make programming and training equivalent. Training develops the underlying capacities; a supplied program directs their exercise in the present work. The freedom to configure that exercise is itself a major productive power.

We can therefore describe the achievement as **semantic programmability**: the ability to configure work over meaningfully organized symbolic material by supplying instructions that the engine can interpret through its learned capacities. This names an operative relationship, not a claim that every possible meaning or instruction already lies within a model's competence.

### 2. New symbolic systems can be introduced through existing ones

The power is not confined to familiar objects with familiar names. An author can introduce an idiosyncratic term, define what kind of object it denotes, establish its relationships and specify how it is to be transformed. An arbitrary name can become usable because the definition connects it to meanings and operations the engine can already handle.

The new symbolic system consequently does not arrive from nowhere. Its entrance is through a pre-existing symbolic system. We use language, notation, examples and already intelligible relationships to constitute the new arrangement. What is unfamiliar in one respect can be made intelligible through what is supplied in another.

For example, a program can introduce a named object consisting of a claim together with the assumptions on which it depends. It can introduce a second kind of object recording the withdrawal of an assumption, and require a transformation that identifies which claim-objects need reconsideration while retaining their identities and their remaining supports. The names of these objects could be entirely unfamiliar. What makes the work intelligible is the account of their constitution, relationships and required treatment.

This is more than teaching the engine synonyms. The definitions establish an organization within which the task makes sense. Change the organization and the appropriate work changes, even if the names remain the same. Conversely, change the names while preserving the definitions and the intended work can remain unchanged.

Novelty can therefore concern both the operands and the operations. We may supply new kinds of object, new relationships among them, new rules for their treatment and new forms of result. Their introduction draws upon the engine's existing capacities, but their particular arrangement need not have been individually encountered during training.

The support supplied by the program matters. A bare invented word does not communicate its intended nature. Nor does an incomplete instruction secretly supply the relationships it omits. Where the arrangement is adequately explained and the task lies within the engine's capacities, however, the existing interpretive power can be configured to work upon it.

**We can make newly defined symbolic organizations actionable without first hand-building a separate interpreter and transformation procedure for every particular use.** That is the opening we must grasp before judging either the engine's limits or its industrial future.

### 3. A stable program can govern variable work

The program and the particular input have different roles, even when both are supplied as text in the same context. The program establishes the responsibility; the input supplies the material upon which that responsibility is exercised on this occasion.

In the example above, the program can remain unchanged while the population of claims, assumptions and withdrawals varies. One input may require no revision. Another may require following several dependencies. A third may expose an uncertainty that the program requires the result to retain rather than resolve by invention. The appropriate sequence of work depends upon the actual composition presented.

This is why the capability cannot be understood simply as selecting an answer from a prepared menu. A defined responsibility can admit many input configurations and require the construction of different outputs. The particular route through the work can vary while the governing purpose remains stable.

The input is consequently not always passive material to which an invariant sequence is applied. Its organization can determine which distinctions become relevant, which operations are needed and how those operations must be combined. A sufficiently capable engine can perform that variable work under a common program.

The magnitude of the achievement lies partly here: a reusable capacity can be configured to handle a class of meaningfully different circumstances. The work need not be individually scripted for every occurrence in order to have a specified purpose and result.

### 4. The engine considered as a programmable constructor

Constructor Theory supplies an economical way to understand this relationship. A constructor is considered through the transformations it can bring about while retaining the capacity to perform its task again. Its subject is a capacity for construction, rather than merely the history of one successful event. A programmable constructor can be configured for different tasks within its repertoire.

Applied here, the operating engine is a reusable transformative apparatus. A program directs it toward a particular responsibility. The supplied input is represented material upon which it works. Its product is a new symbolic construction: an explanation, relation, arrangement, program or other output appropriate to the task.

The realizing apparatus includes the trained model and the computational means through which it executes. The information expressed in the program participates in configuring the construction; its numerical and physical realization makes that configuration effective. We can distinguish those contributions without treating them as independent performances.

This lets us examine the engine as a **practical, approximate programmable constructor of symbolic products**. The qualification concerns the strength of the capability claim. Describing a desired transformation does not establish that a particular engine performs it adequately throughout the intended range. Its actual repertoire must be understood through what it can do under the relevant conditions.

Constructor Theory itself distinguishes ideal capability from imperfect physical realizations. Its usefulness here is not a licence to declare every prompted task solved. It makes the question more definite: what transformation is required, what capacity supplies it, how is that capacity configured, and what remains available for another occurrence?

In ordinary inference, the model's learned parameters need not be changed for each new program or input. The reusable capacity remains available while successive executions produce different results. Retaining that capacity does not require every internal execution state to remain unchanged; it requires the apparatus to retain its ability to perform the task again under the relevant operating conditions.

This account relocates attention from an apparent personality to a constructive relationship. What matters first is the repertoire of transformations, the program that configures their use, the material supplied and the products formed. Questions about general intelligence must then answer to those relationships, rather than replace their examination.

### 5. Symbolic work through a numerical medium

The material is introduced through symbols, but the engine does not need to operate internally as a conventional symbolic interpreter. A typical autoregressive text transformer processes numerical representations and produces successive output tokens through them. This is the computational setting in which the semantic programmability described above is realized.

Input text is divided into tokens, which may be words, parts of words, punctuation or other text units. These are given numerical representations. Through the network's layers, those representations are transformed in ways conditioned by the available sequence and its ordering. Attention is one of the mechanisms through which information from different eligible positions participates in this processing.

The resulting representations contribute to a distribution over possible next tokens. A decoding procedure selects a next token; the selected token becomes part of the sequence used in further generation. Repetition of this process forms an output that may constitute a complex symbolic product.

Calling this a *vector-space realization* identifies the numerical medium through which the processing occurs. It does not mean that every object in a supplied definition occupies one separately identifiable point, or that each rule has been installed as an explicit symbolic law inside the network. Meanings can be expressed through distributed, context-dependent patterns of computation. The actual physical substrate remains the computing apparatus that realizes those numerical operations.

The symbolic and numerical accounts describe connected aspects of the same work. At the symbolic level, we ask for a transformation of defined material. At the computational level, a learned system processes representations conditioned by that material and the instructions. Successful performance connects them: the generated product satisfies what the symbolic task requires.

That connection is not supplied by the mere existence of an output. **The model's distribution over continuations and the task's conditions of success are different things.** A likely continuation is not, by that status alone, a correct inference, an accurate report or a permitted action. A model's learned capacities can produce such results; their adequacy concerns the task that was assigned, not only the mechanism that generated the tokens.

This is also why deterministic decoding does not turn semantic performance into a guarantee. Selecting tokens deterministically can remove one source of variation while leaving the distinction between the generated result and the required result intact. Probabilistic modeling is not simply a synonym for visibly random behavior.

The important achievement remains: learned numerical machinery can be configured through meaningful instructions to perform an extraordinary range of symbolic transformations. Its technical character explains how this power is exercised. It also prepares us to distinguish the power itself from the much larger role it is sometimes imagined to inherit.

## Part II — The future imagined in the image of an entity

### 6. From an interface to an image of the future

An engine that explains, revises and responds is easily encountered as an interlocutor. The interface presents an apparent continuity of attention. It can accept a correction, return to an earlier subject and produce work that resembles the contribution of a collaborator. Human terms become a convenient way of describing the encounter.

The consequential step occurs when that manner of encounter begins to determine the architecture of the imagined future. A useful description of an interaction becomes a picture of what the technology fundamentally is: an entity acquiring more knowledge, more understanding and ultimately a general capacity to take charge of work. Anthropomorphism has then become an architectural expectation. The apparent interlocutor is imagined as the future bearer of the undertaking's responsibilities.

In the strong version of this expectation, improvements converge upon a generally capable intelligence. It can enter an unfamiliar undertaking, discover what matters, acquire the necessary knowledge, organize the work and perform it. The remaining industrial problem is increasingly pictured as providing access, assigning objectives and making the intelligence sufficiently powerful.

We will call this the **entity-centered expectation**. Its defining feature is the concentration of constructive responsibility in the presumed generality of the model. The world supplies problems and access; the increasingly capable entity is expected to supply whatever organization makes the work possible.

The expectation need not require one physical server or one uninterrupted conversation. Many copies could be deployed. Its unity lies in the assumption that the same general intellectual capacity can become the principal answer to an indefinitely widening range of systemic demands.

### 7. Generality becomes a promise about everything around the model

Several distinct claims are compressed into that expectation. Broad performance across tasks is taken to indicate increasingly general competence. General competence is then expected to support increasing autonomy. Increasing autonomy is expected to include the ability to establish the circumstances, knowledge and organization required for work in unfamiliar systems.

The final claim is substantially larger than the first. It concerns not only the engine's performance upon a given task, but its ability to constitute the conditions under which the task can be correctly understood and performed.

This is where the language of general intelligence or superintelligence can cease to explain and begin to conceal. A name for exceptional capacity becomes an advance answer to questions that have not yet been specified. The missing relationship is assigned to future intelligence before its nature has been examined.

Models can improve, and their constructive capacities can contribute to building the systems that employ them. The question is what organization that growth supplies. The ability to perform more tasks and the ability to establish everything those tasks depend upon are different achievements. Moving from one to the other requires an account of the additional work, not simply a stronger adjective before intelligence.

An agentic arrangement is not identical with this promise. A system can initiate and sequence actions within a constituted responsibility without claiming universal adequacy. The object of our examination is the stronger expectation: that enough general intelligence supplies, wherever it goes, the knowledge and organization that would otherwise have to be deliberately established.

That expectation must now be brought into contact with the things it proposes to take over.

## Part III — What the image leaves unresolved

### 8. Knowledge does not end at the model's learned capacities

The engine's acquired capacity to interpret material is not the same thing as the continuing body of knowledge upon which a particular undertaking depends.

Some relevant knowledge may be expressed in its learned parameters. Some may be supplied in context. Other knowledge exists in records, instruments, people, procedures and operating systems. Its subjects continue to change. An event occurs, a requirement is revised, an earlier conclusion loses its premises, or the work itself creates a new object that subsequent work must recognize.

The external world is therefore not merely an additional training collection that has yet to be absorbed. Even if all presently relevant descriptions were available, their relations to changing circumstances would remain part of the work.

Consider a decision supported by an earlier report. Later, one of that report's assumptions is withdrawn. A model may be perfectly capable of interpreting both texts. Yet the productive question is not simply whether the newer text can be read. It concerns which decision relied on which assumption, whether the withdrawal applies to this instance, and which further commitments are affected.

Those relationships belong to the undertaking. They must be available and treated appropriately. A more capable reader can contribute more to establishing them; the need for them is not abolished by the reader's competence.

External knowledge also grows through the creation of meanings and distinctions. A project may introduce a new kind of object, a revised criterion or an operation that did not previously exist. The engine can be instructed in such developments through the symbolic programmability already described. But the development's existence, authority and continuing use are not identical with the model's ability to interpret its description.

**What the engine can understand, what information is supplied to it, and what is warranted in the current undertaking are connected but different achievements.** A conception of the future that compresses them into an increasingly knowledgeable entity has hidden precisely the relationships that must become intelligible.

### 9. Context is supplied; it is not the world itself

The program reaches the engine together with some selected material. That selection matters. It determines which objects, distinctions, observations and commitments are available in the present computation.

An adequate context can let the engine perform work it could not perform from an isolated instruction. An inadequate context can omit the fact or relationship on which the work depends. Conflicting records can require a distinction between versions, subjects or grounds before either record can be used correctly.

Retrieval and memory can help supply this material. Their existence does not settle what should be retrieved, which identity connects records, which statement has been superseded, or what a receiving operation may conclude. These questions can be addressed by the engine, by other means or by their cooperation. In each case there is an actual arrangement through which the responsibility is discharged.

The point is not that a model must contain the world before it can act usefully. The point is that useful action depends upon relationships between the engine and a world it does not constitute merely by describing it. Access is one such relationship. Interpretation is another. Establishing the standing of a result is another.

When these are collapsed into the instruction to understand the situation, the appearance of simplicity is purchased by leaving the construction unspecified. The work has not disappeared. It has been placed inside a phrase.

### 10. The achievement of a system is not automatically a property of its model

The same distinction applies when assessing improvements in capability. Training can change the learned capacities. A different architecture can change how learning and inference are realized. A better supplied context can make a relevant distinction available. A search procedure can explore candidates. An execution environment can establish what a generated program actually does. A checking procedure can reject a result that fails its conditions.

These contributions cooperate, but they are not interchangeable explanations of the result.

If an external record supplies a fact absent from the model's learned representation, the completed answer depends upon that record and the means of obtaining it. If a checker prevents an invalid candidate from becoming a result, its contribution cannot be credited solely to the generator. If a sequence of operations succeeds because intermediate state is retained and supplied appropriately, that organization belongs to the capability being exercised.

Capability belongs to the cooperation of its actual causes. Training contributes the learned powers; the arrangement determines how those powers receive material, perform work and connect with other means. A system may become considerably more capable while the underlying trained model remains unchanged, because that arrangement has changed.

The empirical comparison of these contributions requires specified tasks and evidence. At the conceptual level, however, the consequence is already decisive: **the performance of a complete arrangement does not establish that the model alone possesses the arrangement's powers.**

The entity-centered image can obscure this by treating the developing organization around the model as an increasingly elaborate set of accessories. Yet some of those supposed accessories may carry essential responsibilities. Calling them the intelligence's memory, eyes or tools does not explain away their construction.

### 11. An output, a warranted result and an operative effect

The distinction between an output and a warranted result becomes more consequential when outputs enter further work.

A generated explanation can be examined as a candidate. A generated assertion can instead be accepted as a premise. A generated command can be given access to an operative effect. These uses establish different relationships between the engine's performance and the system in which it participates.

An error in one occurrence can become part of the material for another. Later processing may remain coherent with the supplied material while moving farther from the intended task. The risk concerns how results are carried forward, what standing they acquire and which opportunities exist to establish or repair that standing.

Merely repeating the inference does not supply an independent basis for its correctness. Conversely, an arrangement can include other grounds, observations or constraints that materially change what follows from a mistaken generation. The relevant subject is the organization through which the result becomes consequential.

This is why increasing the model's general competence and creating an adequate productive system are not identical undertakings. A better engine can improve the system. The system still needs the relationships through which the engine's contribution acquires its proper use.

### 12. Electricity: enabling power and the machinery it enables

Electricity gives us a useful way to recover this distinction. Its industrial significance is inseparable from the immense variety of apparatuses that employ it. A supply makes power available; the apparatus determines the work in which that power participates. Making the supply more abundant does not itself specify a motor, a measuring instrument or the organization of a factory.

The transferred relationship is between an enabling power and the organization of its use. The probabilistic engine contributes semantic transformation rather than electrical energy: it is already a complex apparatus with learned capacities, able to perform substantive work upon material and even upon the systems that employ it. Its constructive contribution makes the analogy significant without making the two powers identical.

Nevertheless, its wide applicability does not determine the nature of every system it can help power. Nor does its importance require that those systems be organized in its image. A civilization can depend deeply upon a power while constituting the apparatuses through which that power becomes useful in many different ways.

That is the sense in which the probabilistic engine can become a pervasive enabling supply. Its contribution may occur throughout production, not only at a single conversational interface. What it contributes must be distinguished from the meanings, connections and operating responsibilities of the whole arrangement.

**The more widely a power can be applied, the larger the field of machinery that can be built through it. Breadth of applicability enlarges the work of construction; it does not establish that the power has already performed that work.**

### 13. The danger in an assumed future

Fears about entrusting critical operations to probabilistic inference become intelligible once we specify the arrangement being feared. What may the engine change? What makes an output actionable? Which relationships are assumed to remain correct, and what happens when they do not?

There is something peculiar about treating a hazardous arrangement as the inevitable destination and only then asking how to survive it. Planning to jump from an aircraft without a parachute would indeed present urgent questions. Multiplying the number of people expected to jump would make those questions more urgent, not make the original premise wiser.

The image exposes the prior choice. If the feared future assumes that a general model must become the organizing authority of our productive systems, that assumption belongs among the things to examine. The engine's existence does not command us to assign it that position.

Changing the arrangement can remove a particular path from error to consequence. A proposed change need not acquire operative force merely because it was generated. A task can be bounded by conditions the generator does not get to redefine during its performance. Appropriate independent means can establish particular properties required before a result is used. Which means are adequate depends upon the task and its consequences.

Electricity makes the same relationship familiar in another form. A dangerous power can participate in ordinary life because its use is organized through suitable engineering. The danger does not vanish as a physical fact; the relation between the power and the person or process exposed to it is deliberately constructed.

Our corresponding responsibility is to construct adequate relationships for the powers now available. Calling an arrangement formal would not, by itself, establish those relationships. Nor does rejecting one hazardous premise supply a finished alternative. It does, however, release the future from an unnecessary identification: the technology that enables a system need not be the entity to which the system is surrendered.

The positive question is now unavoidable. If the engine is a power through which future systems can be constructed, what must their construction depend upon? To answer, we have to turn from the image of an intelligence toward the relationship between knowledge and productive means.

## Part IV — Knowledge and the formation of industrial power

### 14. What must be known for a power to become useful

An available power does not specify its own industrial use. Its productive significance depends upon an understanding of what can be done through it, under which conditions, by what arrangements and with what consequences.

That understanding is already operative in the act of designing. To construct a system, we distinguish the things upon which it will work, identify relationships that matter, determine which changes count as accomplishing its purpose, and develop means through which those changes can occur. Some of this work draws upon scientific knowledge. Some draws upon mathematics, logic, accumulated technical experience or deliberately created conceptual instruments.

The epistemic contribution lies in the means through which the subject becomes sufficiently intelligible to support construction. It may involve a representation that makes a dependency visible, a method that permits a consequence to be established, or a distinction without which two importantly different circumstances would be treated as one. Such means do not become technological only after they are placed inside a computer. They are already instruments of capability, with further work required for their particular embodiments.

A material can be available while the knowledge of how to employ it is inadequate. A physical effect can be understood while the apparatus needed for a productive application remains undeveloped. An intellectual instrument can establish a useful relationship while no available system can yet exercise it at the required scale. Each situation contains a different distance between what is possessed and what can be done.

Industrial construction traverses those distances. It does not receive a power and obtain the corresponding industry as an automatic consequence.

### 15. The work beneath the visible industrial achievement

Seen through this relationship, industrial transformation includes changes in what can be understood, represented and established, together with the technical work that makes those achievements productive. The visible machine is one outcome of that development, not an adequate explanation of its own possibility.

The knowledge that makes an intervention intelligible and the means that make it practicable are connected achievements. An explanation of a physical relationship can open a possibility for action; instruments, materials, processes and arrangements must then supply the means of exercising it. Mathematical or conceptual work can likewise establish a useful way of treating a subject, while its operative embodiment demands further construction. The finished apparatus concentrates these achievements into something available for use. Its apparent unity can conceal the depth of the work through which that unity became possible.

The relationship is reciprocal. Practice can expose a distinction that an earlier account failed to make. A new instrument can make phenomena or dependencies accessible that were previously difficult to examine. Technical constraints can redirect conceptual work, while an improved account can open a different technical possibility. Knowledge and its embodiments develop through those encounters.

This matters to the present because the probabilistic engine does not arrive in an empty world. We already possess extensive technologies, bodies of knowledge, instruments and practices. They constitute the actual conditions from which further construction must proceed. Their achievements are resources; their inadequacies, incompatibilities and limits are among the problems to be understood.

Much of the required work can therefore be engineering: creating, adapting and integrating means in response to definite problems, including the epistemic means through which the problems become tractable. A project may require new scientific knowledge, but using established knowledge to construct a new arrangement does not become the discovery of a natural law merely because the arrangement is sophisticated.

The distinction gives the work a more exact responsibility. We need to understand what the problem requires and develop the means that answer it, rather than wait for an undifferentiated advance called intelligence to dissolve every difficulty at once.

### 16. The question now prepared

The achievement with which we began can participate on both sides of this relationship. It can help us develop concepts, representations, programs and arrangements. It can also supply transformative power within the arrangements that result. Its importance concerns both the construction of productive means and their continuing operation.

The connection begins with the programmability established in Part I. Definitions of objects, relationships and operations can configure reusable transformative work. Their precision can therefore have a productive consequence: it changes what can be asked of the engine, what its products must preserve and how those products can enter further activity. Developing the meanings is already part of developing the capability.

An instruction interpreted on one occasion is not yet a formal constitution governing a continuing system. Moving toward the latter requires exact accounts of the relevant relationships and means that actually realize them. But the new power gives that development both a wider use and an additional means of being undertaken. It can help construct the accounts and participate in the work they organize. This is the specific opening toward formal development, beyond the general observation that powerful tools improve engineering.

The passage still has to be constructed. Its significance is that what is defined can become consequential to what is done, across more than the immediate generation of one answer. The necessary relationship between meaning, formal organization and operation is the next subject to develop—not an achievement we obtain merely by naming it.

We have therefore reached the foundation of the formal industrial question. The choice is not between admiring the engine and recognizing the work beyond it. Its power makes that work more consequential. The future can be approached as the growth of an entity presumed to supply every missing capacity, or as the deliberate development of the knowledge and machinery through which enlarged powers become industrially effective.

The Formal Industrial Revolution takes the second direction. Its fuller nature must now be developed from the relationship established here: **a new transformative power, the epistemic means through which its possibilities become intelligible, and the technical construction through which those possibilities acquire operative form.**

The next question is what it would mean for formal development to become a deeper productive force across that relationship. We have not answered it by naming it. We have established why it must be asked—and why the engine, however powerful, is the beginning of the industrial undertaking rather than its completed form.
