## Removed passage — as supplied

In this essay I will argue that the future of our civilization, of industrial knowledge creation and of the productive capacities built upon it will depend upon our ability to develop increasingly powerful formal explanatory systems, both the development of epistemic techne as well as their embodiment in computing systems and auxiliary technologies. That entails substantial semantic and ontological expansion, the integration or adaptation of mathematical instruments into new ones, and the bridging of all those into the operable substrates required to make these advances productive. Its prospect is an industrial civilization whose powers grow through the deliberate development of the intellectual means by which further powers can be conceived and constructed. I call this undertaking the Formal Industrial Revolution.

The formal development of a productive domain can become a means of creating its industrial machinery. This requires far more than a rigorous description of existing practice. It requires an intellectual constitution of possible work, together with technologies able to give that constitution operative force. An advance in these means could alter the range of systems that can be designed and brought into operation, and thereby change which industries we are capable of creating. The proposal is to extend this relationship through the depth and diversity of our productive knowledge, including domains whose adequate intellectual organization has yet to be developed.

The philosophical ambition has a long ancestry. The early-modern expansion of our understanding of nature changed humanity's material horizon: explanations and mathematical instruments made further technologies conceivable, while technical advances supplied new powers and problems for inquiry. Bacon's vision helped give organized inquiry a productive horizon: people could work together toward knowledge and capabilities that present practice did not yet possess. The Enlightenment widened the ambition to transform human circumstances through intellectual development. I will argue that the common philosophical responsibility for this ambition was progressively relinquished during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, even as its powers developed magnificently in mathematics, science and later in computing. Renewing it means taking responsibility for possibilities that this immense, dispersed inheritance now places before us.

Our empirical condition includes that inheritance: existing technologies, organized knowledge, established practices and the constraints they encounter. Alongside natural limits, we encounter limitations belonging to intellectual and technical means that we have ourselves developed. Their abundance does not mean we possess adequate instrumentarium for every undertaking they make conceivable. A new productive ambition may require an explanatory framework unavailable as a ready to use instrument in any of the disciplines from which we might draw. The technologies can exist while an adequate conception of their possible cooperation does not. An industrial limitation may therefore lie in how its subject can be understood and developed, long before it becomes a shortage of computational or physical power.

Here semantic and ontological development acquires its industrial importance. What a field allows us to distinguish, explain and connect helps determine which problems can be formulated and which constructions can be conceived. Expanding those means can bring a previously inaccessible class of undertakings into view. Much of present knowledge also depends upon cultivated human understanding that its written accounts do not exhaust. Making such knowledge more explicitly operable requires substantial intellectual work; future capabilities may require distinctions and explanations that nobody has yet developed. We must be able both to reconstruct an inheritance and to create beyond it.

Mathematics offers an enormous instrumentarium for this work. Its industrial significance must be developed through its relationships with the problems before us. Existing instruments can be adapted to newly constituted subjects; a more general framework can make connections intelligible across otherwise separate organizations of knowledge. Such developments can yield specialized epistemic tools, or instruments that make many different domains approachable. Further mathematical creation may become necessary where the existing means prove inadequate. The achievement is an expansion of what can be investigated and constructed, including the means of further investigation and construction. Its value cannot be measured solely by how much faster it performs a familiar task.

The word *formal* identifies a decisive part of this direction. The intellectual means themselves become explicit constructions whose consequences, possibilities and limits can be investigated systematically. Their laws can establish what remains valid across families of admissible constructions, including cases never individually enumerated. When appropriate technologies preserve and exercise that organization, formal development can shape the causal constitution of productive machinery. Computing supplies an immense precedent for this relationship. The undertaking is to extend it through increasingly rich semantic and ontological systems, rather than treat today's readily computable subjects as the final limits of what can be made operative.

This requires epistemic engineering and technological development across their entire connection. A new intellectual instrument may demand representational or computational powers that no existing system adequately supplies. Creating those powers can require further epistemic instruments and new computational foundations, rather than extensions of the systems already available. Attempted realization can also expose deficiencies in the originating conception. What we can conceive, what we can formally develop and what we can realize change one another's demands. The work is neither completed in philosophy before engineering begins nor confined to what present engineering already permits. Its progression includes creation, adaptation and integration at every level where the intended capability requires them.

Domain modelling follows from this undertaking. Its purpose is to develop the intellectual organization adequate to a productive field, including fields that cross or transform inherited divisions of knowledge. Methods capable of realizing such models must develop alongside them. Where those methods faithfully realize what a model defines, the model can supply the organization from which operative arrangements are derived. Its development then participates directly in creating the means of production. An epistemic advance can be carried into the resulting capability through its construction, rather than depending wholly on practitioners reinterpreting that advance for each new application.

Industrial expansion could then reach into the production of its own epistemic means. We could build capabilities for developing and comparing formal models, exploring their consequences, and constructing the systems through which they become operative. Such capabilities would themselves be products of prior intellectual and technological development. Their exercise could furnish subsequent work with new means of conceiving, examining and realizing further productive arrangements. New problems would be confronted with instruments that preceding productive achievements had made possible, while their demands would direct further creation. The intellectual conditions of industrial formation would increasingly become something civilization could deliberately produce and improve. Here lies the proposed revolution: extending the relationship between formal conception and operative power until the systematic development of that relationship becomes a principal way of creating industries and expanding what their successors can undertake.

That expansion places a more explicit responsibility upon us for our own fallibility. An account can be formally coherent and still inadequate to its intended subject; a sound conception can be badly realized. In claiming a productive capability, we must specify what is to be accomplished and develop the means through which failure to accomplish it can be exposed. Engineering brings these claims into contact with actual circumstances. Its failures may demand correction in the machine, in the model or in the original understanding of the problem. Formality should make more of these commitments and dependencies examinable, connecting a failure to the intellectual or technical work needed to address it.

The result is an undertaking whose advances can alter the frontier on which further work occurs. Overcoming one limitation can furnish the means to confront a problem that previously lay beyond our powers. Sustaining this development requires institutions that cultivate work across inherited disciplinary divisions, and education through which new epistemic instruments become usable beyond their original specialists. Industrial development must include the creation and revision of these intellectual foundations, together with the technical powers through which their consequences can be pursued.

The twenty-first century should make this a principal undertaking for the twenty-second and beyond: to develop the epistemic and technological foundations from which further industries can be constituted. The inheritance we leave should enlarge our successors' powers of creation, not merely the inventory of what they can operate. That requires sustained work on knowledge itself, on its formal means of development and on the systems through which those means become productive. Greater industrial output would be one measure of success; the deeper achievement would be a civilization increasingly capable of creating its own productive possibilities.
