# The Formal Industrial Revolution

## Introduction

We now possess, in transformer-based language models, a technological power of extraordinary scope: probabilistic engines programmable over our symbolic systems, including new systems of objects, relations and operations defined through those already available. Their industrial significance reaches beyond the tasks they can perform today. They give us means of working within organizations of knowledge and production that we have yet to construct. The question is what we will make through this power—and what kind of future we mistake it for having already supplied.

The distinction separates two conceptions of the future. In one, the principal object of development is an intelligence: increasingly general, increasingly autonomous, eventually capable of assuming whatever responsibilities we place before it. In the other, the breakthrough supplies a transformative power through which we can develop and operate new productive systems. Their constitution becomes the central work. The intelligence of an instrument is no longer asked to stand for the organization of an industry.

**The Formal Industrial Revolution names this second undertaking: the deliberate development of the epistemic and technical means through which productive capabilities can be formally constituted, realized and extended.** What a system works upon, which transformations it can perform, what relationships its work must preserve, and how its results become available to further construction become connected objects of design. Formal development reaches into the productive capability itself. It gives operative form to distinctions, dependencies and possibilities that would otherwise remain distributed across descriptions, institutions, practitioners and their unrecorded interpretive work.

The probabilistic engine makes this undertaking newly consequential in two directions. It offers means of undertaking the immense work of definition, connection and construction; it also offers a flexible transformative capacity that the resulting organizations can employ. Greater explicitness can therefore become more than a cost incurred in documenting what people already know. It can enlarge what a productive system is able to do. The creation of formal means and the development of their uses can begin to reinforce one another.

This reaches considerably deeper than adding an intelligent interface to existing machinery. An intended capability may demand new conceptual instruments, new representations, new ways of maintaining their relationships, or new computational arrangements through which those relationships acquire effect. Some requirements can be met by integrating what already exists; others will expose deficiencies in the available means. The work proceeds across that entire connection. A representation may change the machinery it requires, while the attempt to realize it may reveal something that the representation failed to distinguish.

Industry has always depended upon more than the visible apparatus that performs its work. Scientific understanding, mathematical instruments, technical judgment and the organization of production participate in what that apparatus makes possible. Our present opportunity concerns the further development of these relationships: making more of their intellectual constitution an explicit and operable part of productive systems, while creating the means needed to sustain it. The scale of the undertaking is industrial; much of its decisive work is epistemic.

The longer horizon follows from that depth. The twenty-first century should be understood as a period in which we assume responsibility for the foundations on which the twenty-second will build. The powers now becoming available do not relieve us of that responsibility. They make a more ambitious exercise of it possible. This account examines the transformation in that light: from the power we have acquired, through the future mistakenly assigned to it, toward the knowledge, constructions and institutions through which another future can be made.

## Part I — The breakthrough and the future assigned to it

### 1. A programmable constructor over symbolic systems

The extraordinary achievement of the probabilistic engine is a form of programmability in which a semantic specification can organize computational work. We can define the operands, prescribe how they are to be considered, specify transformations and require a particular organization of the result. Within its operative competence, the engine can carry out work configured in this way without our separately implementing every task-specific interpreter and procedure.

Its significance becomes clearest where the operands and operations are new. Existing symbolic resources can be used to constitute another symbolic organization: objects with deliberately assigned natures, relations that do not correspond to an established disciplinary vocabulary, and rules under which those objects must be combined, transformed or assessed. The existing language supplies the means of defining the new system; the engine's program supplies how work is to proceed within it. Novel names are incidental. The substantive achievement is that **newly defined meanings and relationships can become the organizing terms of a transformation**.

This permits more than the execution of a separately described operation on each occasion. A stable program can address a class of variable populations. Which objects are present, how they are related and what state they occupy can determine which of several transformations is appropriate and how further work must be composed. The required output is specified through the task; its particular structure is produced in response to the actual input. We can thus configure a repertoire of conditional work rather than enumerate its every occurrence.

Constructor Theory provides a useful perspective on this capacity. A constructor is characterized by transformations it can bring about while retaining the capacity to act again; a programmable constructor admits different tasks through a program.[^constructor] The operating engine can be considered in those terms: a reusable, approximate constructor of symbolic products, whose program and input organization help determine the construction performed. Its generality lies in the range of work that can be configured through it. The adequacy of a particular configuration remains a property to establish over the circumstances in which it is meant to operate.

The underlying computation does not thereby become a conventional symbolic interpreter. In an autoregressive transformer, numerical representations are transformed through the network and used to produce distributions over continuations; decoding realizes a particular output sequence. What we specify in symbolic terms is carried through this vector-valued computation. A newly introduced relation need not become an individually encoded primitive, and a prescribed rule does not become an inviolable law of the mechanism merely because it appears in the program. The distinction is consequential: semantic programmability gives us access to a transformative capacity, not an automatic identity between the requested operation and every result produced.

The resulting capacity is a profound release from task-by-task construction. Much that previously required a specially developed computational treatment can now be attempted through the adequate constitution of a task and its operands. This includes work upon the definitions themselves: distinguishing, connecting, revising and extending the organization within which subsequent transformations will occur. We possess a power that can participate both in making a symbolic system and in working through it. That double capacity—not the conversational form in which it is commonly encountered—is the opening of industrial significance.

### 2. When generality is made to carry an entire future

The entity-centered conception of the future takes a different lesson from this breadth. The model appears as a general intellectual agent; improvements become steps toward a superior entity; industrial development becomes the progressive admission of that entity into more of our affairs. Give it sufficient intelligence, context, access and time, and it is expected to discover and discharge whatever an undertaking requires.

The consequential anthropomorphism is architectural, not merely linguistic. It matters little whether a useful shorthand calls a computation thinking. It matters greatly when the image of a thinker determines where we place the responsibilities of the system. The expectation at issue is that a sufficiently general model can become the standing answer to requirements that have not yet been identified, let alone developed.

Its apparent economy comes from compressing several achievements into one. Competence at a widening range of tasks is made to imply competence at discovering their prerequisites, constituting their meanings, maintaining the relevant world of objects, and establishing what makes their results adequate. These additional achievements may themselves be assisted by the engine. Their actual construction, however, is precisely what the expectation leaves obscure. The work is assigned to an anticipated intelligence before we have understood the nature of the work.

This is not a dispute over whether future models can exceed present ones. It is a dispute over what their excellence would accomplish. A model that helps create an indispensable representation has contributed to a new system; the representation and its operative relationships do not cease to matter because a model helped produce them. A model that helps construct its own means of verification has enlarged the machinery of its use; that machinery does not become dispensable because its origin was partly probabilistic. **The more effectively the engine helps build what is missing, the more important it becomes to recognize what has actually been built.**

The distinction changes what counts as progress. Under the entity-centered expectation, developments around the model are easily interpreted as accessories to an intelligence whose eventual generality will subsume their responsibilities. Under the industrial conception, they can be beginnings of the productive organization itself. The question is no longer how nearly the model resembles an agent competent at everything. It is which capabilities the resulting construction possesses, what sustains them, and how they can be composed into further capabilities.

### 3. The undertaking has a world that the engine must work within

The knowledge relevant to productive work is not simply a quantity of information awaiting absorption into a model. An undertaking creates and changes its own objects. A design acquires a revision; a requirement becomes binding; a dependency is withdrawn; a result establishes something that subsequent work may use. Their significance depends upon relationships that belong to the undertaking and must continue to hold through its transformations.

A revised design, for example, is not merely newer text. It has an identity relative to its predecessor, consequences for dependent work, and a particular standing among alternative proposals and accepted commitments. Interpreting its contents and maintaining those relationships are different contributions to the same productive responsibility. The engine can assist both. Neither is supplied merely by increasing the volume of material it can process.

The difficulty therefore extends beyond retrieving the right passage. It concerns the constitution of what there is to retrieve, which distinctions the records express, how changes propagate, and what makes a result authoritative for another operation. These are simultaneously epistemic and architectural questions. A system cannot maintain a distinction that its representation never makes; an impeccable distinction in a document cannot govern operations that do not preserve it. The relation between the two is part of the system's construction.

This becomes more important, not less, when we exercise the ability to define new symbolic organizations. Their meanings will not always be inherited from an established field or fixed before development begins. We may create a relation because a new form of work requires it, then discover consequences that demand further objects or operations. Knowledge is growing through the undertaking. Its productive organization must develop with it, while preserving enough continuity for work already performed to remain intelligible.

The engine's context is one point of access to that organization. Context selection can express substantive knowledge about relevance, identity, dependencies and the present state of work. Treating it as a mere accommodation to a model's limited capacity misses this constructive role. Even an engine able to process a vastly greater volume would still need the relationships by which different materials concern the same thing, contradict one another, supersede one another or bear on different responsibilities.

The same distinction governs our interpretation of capability gains. A fixed model can become more useful through a better representation, access to relevant state, a suitable execution environment or a procedure that establishes a required property of its output. The resulting achievement belongs to their cooperation. Crediting the whole to the model converts evidence for the importance of systemic construction into evidence for the sufficiency of a general entity.

Our task is to make that cooperation explicit enough to develop deliberately. The productive world is not an inconvenient quantity of context surrounding the engine. It is what the engine is being employed to work upon, within, and sometimes to help extend.

### 4. Enabling power is not governing authority

Electricity offers the appropriate change of perspective. Its extraordinary generality appears in the diversity of the machinery it powers. We do not expect the source of electrical power to supply the constitution of each machine. Its industrial significance consists partly in how much can be constructed once that source is available.

The probabilistic engine supplies transformation rather than energy. It can help design the machinery in which it participates, which makes its contribution more deeply constructive than the analogy's physical counterpart. The governing relationship nevertheless holds: **a pervasive enabling power need not be the organizing authority of the systems it enables**. Its availability can enlarge the field of possible machinery rather than converge upon one entity-like machine to which every undertaking is delegated.

This changes the interpretation of danger. If a probabilistic sequence is permitted to establish its own premises, judge its own success and carry its outputs directly into consequential operations, errors can propagate through a system that continues to appear coherent on its own terms. The concern is real. But treating that arrangement as the unavoidable future is like deciding that we shall jump from an aircraft without parachutes and then devoting our ingenuity to the difficulties of the descent. The first question concerns the premise of the undertaking.

A different constitution assigns the engine particular transformations within an organization that determines what their inputs mean, which effects they may acquire, and how those effects relate to subsequent work. These are productive relationships, not merely restraints. They establish the objects upon which work is possible, the transformations that accomplish something, and the conditions under which one accomplishment creates the occasion or material for another. The engine's powers can be distributed throughout such an organization without each occurrence having to reconstruct the authority and meaning of the whole.

The formal task begins there: to make those relationships operative in the machinery itself. Relevant objects and states must have representations through which their identities, dependencies and admissible transformations can be maintained. A generated product must enter further work with the properties that work requires; a change in the represented undertaking must have its defined consequences for what becomes possible or necessary next. These requirements constitute productive organization. A checker placed after an otherwise undefined activity supplies only a fraction of it.

Control must be established at the relationships where effects become consequential. Formal guarantees extend to what has actually been specified and realized; correspondence with external processes has to be maintained through appropriate observation and engineering. Those responsibilities belong to the construction, as insulation and protection belong to the engineering through which electricity becomes usable. Rejecting one hazardous architecture does not make every alternative adequate. It makes the architecture a subject of deliberate work instead of a fate imposed by the existence of the engine.

The resulting future need not contain less probabilistic computation. It may contain vastly more, exercised through a multiplicity of constituted productive arrangements. The decisive change is where coherence resides: in a developing system whose relationships we undertake to define and realize, rather than in the expectation that the next inference will supply whatever the previous ones left unspecified.

### 5. The industrial consequence of an epistemic power

The passage from a new power to an industry is a passage through knowledge and construction. Industrial means become possible through ways of identifying a phenomenon, representing a relationship, calculating an effect or organizing a process—and through the machinery capable of giving those achievements productive consequence. Much of what appears in the finished apparatus as a technical feature began as a distinction someone had to make, a relationship someone had to establish, or a possibility someone had to formulate.

This is the connection through which previous industrial transformations must be examined. Their visible technologies were consequences of deeper developments, but not products of a simple procession from theory to application. Technical practice also supplied problems, instruments and discoveries through which knowledge advanced. The relation is reciprocal: what can be understood changes what can be built; what can be built changes what can be investigated and understood. An adequate account of industrial development must follow that movement rather than begin and end with its most conspicuous machines.

Our present situation places unusual weight on the epistemic side of this relationship. We already possess immense bodies of knowledge and extensive technical capacities. Yet their availability does not establish their readiness for the forms of joint operation now becoming possible. A field may sustain highly accomplished practice while leaving essential distinctions and transitions to the practitioner. Several adequate accounts may prove insufficient when their objects must participate in one system. A representation may express the distinctions while the available software cannot maintain or transform them in the required way.

These are productive problems in their own right. Addressing them can require conceptual, semantic, mathematical or logical work, together with changes in the systems through which that work is exercised. The difficulty may lie in an instrument that does not yet exist, an existing instrument unsuited to the intended combination, or a missing passage between already adequate means. Its resolution can demand work at several levels at once. Formalizing an account and subsequently arranging its implementation is too shallow a picture of this undertaking.

The probabilistic breakthrough changes the situation because it can operate upon the symbolic means through which these problems are developed. It can participate in articulating a new distinction, constructing a representation, deriving consequences, producing code, or adapting material to the requirements of another operation. Each contribution must acquire the adequacy its role requires. The significant opening is that the same class of transformative power can also be employed *within* the resulting organization. The work of constituting that organization has an enlarged productive destination.

Here the two directions introduced at the beginning meet. **Formal development becomes more practicable through the new means available to undertake it; it becomes more valuable through the new means available to operate what it defines.** This is more than a reduction in the cost of producing descriptions. It is the possibility of making explicit intellectual constructions into sustained productive capacities: arrangements in which defined objects and their changing relationships determine what can be done, how accomplishments connect, and what further construction becomes possible.

The intended expansion concerns the scope and depth of what becomes operable. A definition can enter the constitution of a capability: which variations of an object the system can handle, which combinations have meaning, and which transformations can produce a result available to another process. Extending that constitution can enlarge the range of products the system can make, rather than merely add another description to its records. The engine supplies a flexible means of performing work within that range; the formal and technical organization gives the range its determinate, continuing existence.

No inference engine, by its availability alone, establishes those relationships. Yet an engine programmable over newly constituted symbolic organizations gives us a powerful means of realizing them. Its importance lies precisely in the scale of constructive work it can enable. To make it the presumed substitute for that work would be to mistake the opening of an industrial possibility for its completion.

The next problem is therefore not simply how to improve the engine. It is how to develop the knowledge and machinery of the productive orders that this power makes possible: how their subjects must be modelled, what must become formal, which epistemic instruments their construction requires, and what technical embodiments can carry them. Those questions locate the work ahead. They are where the Formal Industrial Revolution begins to acquire its own subject.
