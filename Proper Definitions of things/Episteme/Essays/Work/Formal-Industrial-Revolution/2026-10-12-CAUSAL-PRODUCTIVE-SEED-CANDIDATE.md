# The Formal Industrial Revolution

## Introduction

We have acquired a computational power that can be programmed over our symbolic systems—and, through them, over new symbolic organizations we deliberately define. Its industrial significance reaches well beyond the automation of familiar tasks. It can help develop the objects, relationships and instruments through which unfamiliar tasks become possible, and then participate in performing them. The same class of power can contribute to the construction of a productive capability and to its exercise.

The central question is where that capability will reside. One conception of the future places it principally in the expanding general competence of the probabilistic engine: a progressively more capable entity to which ever larger undertakings can be entrusted. The conception developed here places it in the productive systems that become possible to constitute. Their substance lies in the knowledge, formal objects, causal relationships, constructive means and operative environments through which a class of work can actually be accomplished. Probabilistic transformation is a major enabling power within that organization. It is not its entire constitution, nor its only transformative means.

**The Formal Industrial Revolution names the proposed transformation of our productive order through the development and operation of such formally constituted capabilities.** Its ambition is to make increasingly deep organizations of knowledge productive: to give their objects and relationships operative consequences, to realize their transformations, and to compose their achievements into further means of production. Causal organization takes the central place that the entity-centered account assigns to general probabilistic competence.

This demands much more than translating existing descriptions into software. The relevant distinctions may be missing from the descriptions; the formal instruments may need adaptation or development; the available systems may be unable to represent or exercise what the work requires. Epistemic construction and technical construction must therefore proceed together, sometimes through the foundations of both. The result sought is not simply more output from the means we already possess, but a greater capacity to constitute new productive means.

The industrial claim is correspondingly larger than a claim about computation. It concerns how capabilities become reproducible, combinable and extensible parts of economic and social life; how knowledge acquires productive consequence; and how a civilization deliberately enlarges what it can make and accomplish. The twenty-first century can be approached as the period in which we undertake the foundations for that expansion, rather than wait for an anticipated intelligence to supply them. What follows develops the initial grounds of this position. Its further historical, institutional and technical responsibilities remain open to construction.

## Part I — The power and the future attributed to it

### 1. Programmable construction over symbolic systems

The power we now possess is a probabilistic engine that can undertake transformations over our symbolic systems without our first implementing a procedure specific to each such transformation. We supply the operands, their meaning, the work required and the relationships the result must satisfy. Within its capabilities, the engine can act on that semantic specification. The distinctive gain lies in the substantive work that can be configured through the specification itself.

The significance becomes sharper when the operands themselves are new. A symbolic system can be introduced through another: novel objects can receive defined natures; relations can be constituted for a particular purpose; operations can be specified over combinations that have no established disciplinary name. The original language supplies the means of defining the new organization. It is the defined meaning, not the familiarity of its labels, that makes work within it intelligible.

For classes of work within its reach, a supplied programme can replace substantial task-specific development. New distinctions can be introduced, their implications explored and transformations attempted before a specialized computational treatment has been built for them. The same engine can help develop the specification and work on the populations it admits. This changes the practical cost of approaching a new organization of meaning: we have a means of working within it while its more exact instruments and realizations are still being constituted.

A stable programme can also address variable compositions. Its input may contain different objects, relationships and states on successive occasions, while its instructions specify how to respond to those differences. The programme determines a repertoire of possible work; the particular composition contributes to determining what work is performed. A fixed responsibility can therefore produce different transformations and products without being rewritten for every occurrence.

Constructor Theory supplies a useful perspective: a constructor has the capacity to accomplish a task while retaining its capacity to act again; a programmable constructor admits different tasks through a programme.[^constructor] Applied here, the engine is a reusable, approximate means of symbolic construction. Its configuration, operands and surrounding apparatus jointly determine what productive responsibility it can discharge. Calling it a constructor directs attention to that capacity, rather than to the appearance of an interlocutor.

The symbolic specification is carried through a different computational substrate. In an autoregressive transformer, tokens and context receive numerical representations; parameterized vector-valued operations contribute to distributions over continuations; decoding produces an output sequence. A relation defined in the programme does not thereby become an individually implemented primitive, and an instruction does not acquire the status of a mechanically enforced rule merely by being stated. Deterministic decoding changes the selection of the continuation, not the grounds for identifying it with the intended transformation.

That distinction locates the achievement without diminishing it. We have a highly flexible way to configure computational work over symbolic organizations, including ones newly constituted for the occasion. We can employ it to develop definitions, construct representations, explore consequences, produce technical realizations and undertake transformations within the resulting arrangements. **Its double contribution—to making productive organizations and to working through them—is what gives the breakthrough its industrial depth.**

### 2. General competence and the constitution it cannot stand in for

The entity-centered promise can be stated without caricature. A sufficiently capable general model, supplied with suitable tools, context and access, is expected increasingly to determine what an undertaking requires and carry it through. Supporting infrastructure improves its reach; further gains in competence are expected to absorb responsibilities that presently need separate human or technical arrangements.

The consequential step is not the prediction of improvement. It is the promotion of improvement into an explanation of industrial sufficiency. What must be represented, what counts as an accomplishment, which relationships must persist, and which transformations can make the intended outcome real are treated as matters the future entity will somehow discharge by virtue of its general competence.

These responsibilities do not disappear when a model becomes able to help discharge them. If it creates a necessary representation, that representation becomes part of the productive means. If it constructs an exact procedure, the procedure contributes its own power. If it establishes a mechanism through which changing circumstances become available to an operation, that mechanism is part of the system's capacity. Their origin does not dissolve their subsequent necessity.

The strongest version of the promise may therefore include an engine that builds the machinery it needs. That would be an extraordinary constructor of productive systems. The consequential question would become what its construction had established: which meanings and responsibilities now persist in the apparatus, which depend on renewed interpretation, and which powers have actually become available. A capability embodied in a durable operative relationship is different from a capacity to attempt its reconstruction whenever the next occasion arises. The distinction concerns where productive competence resides, not who originally supplied it.

Anthropomorphism matters here at the level of architecture. A human-like name is incidental; an entity-like allocation of responsibility is not. When the imagined intelligence becomes the standing answer to unspecified requirements, it conceals the very work by which a productive capability must come into existence. Generality names a range of possible contribution. It does not identify what has been accomplished in a particular industrial constitution.

The associated expectation that sufficient improvement will somehow overcome the engine's probabilistic character compresses another unresolved passage. Better performance is not, by itself, an argument establishing that every admissible case will receive its required treatment. A reliable relation between specification and realization requires grounds appropriate to that relation. Where exact procedures, formally established constraints or other constructions supply those grounds, their contribution belongs in the explanation of the result.

This is not an impossibility theorem about every conceivable future architecture. It is a precise objection to substituting an anticipated degree of competence for the constitution of the responsibility itself. An engine may help construct an adequate answer. Neither its generality nor its designation as an intelligence establishes that the answer has already been supplied.

Electricity provides the relevant change of perspective. Its industrial significance is realized through a diversity of machines whose organization it does not itself provide. The probabilistic engine contributes transformations rather than energy, and can also participate in designing the apparatus that employs it. Yet the central relation remains: the availability of a pervasive enabling power enlarges the field of systems that can be built. It does not make their construction incidental.

On this account, the engine's future importance may grow enormously while its place becomes more precisely determined. The productive whole, rather than the presumed sufficiency of an entity, becomes the subject of industrial development.

## Part II — The constitution of causal productive capability

### 3. What formal organization must make possible

An undertaking does not consist of undifferentiated information. It has subjects whose identities matter, relationships that license particular uses, conditions under which work becomes applicable, and transformations through which something determinate is accomplished. Those distinctions can be supplied through professional practice, human judgment and technical arrangements without having been constituted together as an explicit operative whole. Their tacit availability to an accomplished practitioner is not equivalent to their availability within a machine.

A domain model, in the productive sense required here, must reach beyond a vocabulary or record structure. It must constitute the relevant subjects of work and the relationships through which that work has meaning: what can vary, which combinations count, what operations can accomplish, and what makes one product available to another undertaking. The domain's defining effort is therefore partly the construction of its possible work, not only a description of its existing contents.[^domain]

Some limitations cannot be repaired by applying more inference to the same representation. Let a representation \(r\) give an operation its access to situations \(s\), and let \(q(s)\) be the required answer. A procedure \(g\) working only through that representation can recover the answer exactly, \(q=g\circ r\), only if

\[
r(s_1)=r(s_2)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad q(s_1)=q(s_2).
\]

If the implication fails, the representation has discarded a distinction on which the answer depends. A probabilistic procedure supplied with the same represented input has no additional basis for selecting differently between those situations; without further information it cannot guarantee the different answers. More capable computation may improve a guess under additional assumptions. It does not recover an absent distinction by virtue of its power alone.

The constructive response can require a new object, a richer relation, another observation or a different epistemic instrument. This is not an argument for recording everything. It is an argument for constituting the distinctions the responsibility actually needs. Changing the responsibility can consequently change what must be represented.

Composition exposes further demands. Two assessments may each be correctly formed, yet their joint use may be invalid because they concern different revisions, different criteria or incompatible circumstances. Making their value types compatible does not make their subjects identical. A meaningful composite requires the context under which its constituents can cooperate. Defining that context is part of the productive construction, not an annotation added after the data have been joined.

Formal organization becomes substantive at precisely these points. It makes the relevant relationships, transformation effects and conditions of composition explicit and available for realization. Whether we can rely on them depends on the adequacy of that account and of the machinery exercising it. Formality is not exhausted by putting the description into a precise notation: the notation must express the relevant constitution, and that constitution must support the operations being claimed.

This can require developing knowledge rather than merely transcribing it. A new class of work may expose a distinction that existing practice never needed to make explicit, an intermediate subject that no contributing field had to constitute, or a joint relationship that disappears when several accounts are combined. The formal task is to develop those missing means.

The operative counterpart must then exist. A transformation named in a definition needs constructive means; a represented dependency needs mechanisms through which its consequences are maintained; a product intended for further use needs a realization that preserves what that use requires. A formal account without those powers defines a responsibility still to be supplied. Machinery that ignores the account does not supply it by producing superficially suitable output.

The connection is stronger where reusable realizing powers already exist. A sufficiently complete definition can determine the operative arrangement by interpretation, generation or another faithful realization, without requiring its meaning to be reconstructed during implementation. Where the defining forms exceed those powers, further machinery is needed. In either case, the domain's conceptual depth is made available to productive construction.

### 4. Causal organization as generative machinery

A formal object becomes productive through the consequences its constitution acquires in an operative system. Its identity determines which relationships it can enter; its composition determines what can be selected or transformed; its state participates in conditions that make further work applicable. When that work produces a new object or changes an existing one, the resulting configuration can become the basis of another construction.

This is a causal organization of production, not merely an inventory inspected by an external intelligence. The relationships already constituted in the apparatus have operative consequences without needing to be reconstructed by each successive inference. Where interpretation is required, it has its own place and responsibility within that organization; it does not inherit the undefined responsibility for deciding what the whole must be.

The causal model and the causal machine are therefore two sides of an obligation of correspondence. The model makes the intended organization explicit; the operative mechanisms realize the relevant dependencies and transformations. A model is not the physical cause of an effect merely because it describes it. It becomes part of productive machinery through a realization that gives its defined relationships the corresponding operative force.[^constructive]

The scope can extend through several kinds of constructor. Some contributions require exact computation or structural transformation. Others require search, interpretation, interaction or a configured probabilistic operation. Different realizations can serve related responsibilities, and the same underlying engine can participate through many distinct programmes. What matters is the constructive role each supplies and the conditions under which the roles compose.

Such an organization can contain very large numbers of narrowly specified constructive roles. Each receives a particular context, performs work defined for that context and yields products that enter specified relationships. The number of roles is not the number of separately trained models; it is the differentiation of productive responsibilities. Broad capability need not be concentrated in a single broadly empowered operation.

Nor does specificity imply a fixed production line. A particular constructor's programme can respond to variable populations; different relationships among its operands can call for different transformations. At the level of the whole, products can become further operands, satisfy new conditions, expose a conflict, supply an intermediate construction or constitute another available means. The resulting activity can be generated by the actual state of the productive world rather than selected from a list of complete histories written in advance.

This is how boundedness and generativity belong together. A finite constitution can specify kinds of object and operations of composition through which arbitrarily many finite constructions are admitted. The boundary limits the nature of the productive capability; it need not limit the number of instances to a finite catalogue. Actual operation still requires resources appropriate to the instance.

The capability claim must concern that independently defined class. It cannot be rescued by redefining the class after execution as whatever happened to succeed. If the machine claims to accomplish a given task throughout its admitted range, its objects, methods and realizing powers must support that range under the stated operating premises. Naming a possibility space or a search procedure does not establish that the required route to a result is available.

There is also a decisive difference between a new instance and a new kind of responsibility. A fresh configuration within the admitted space belongs to the machine's ordinary productive work. A requirement that needs distinctions, transformations or powers outside that constitution demands an extension. An apparatus may itself be designed to construct some such extensions, but that is a further capability to establish, not an automatic consequence of calling the system generative.

Causality does not certify any of this as good. A machine can realize an inadequate model exactly, preserve the wrong distinctions or pursue a mistaken purpose with great consistency. Causal constitution makes the operative relationships a subject of deliberate design and examination. Adequacy concerns whether those relationships and their realization accomplish what the machine owes.

### 5. Epistemic engineering through the whole apparatus

The depth of this undertaking cannot be captured by a succession in which knowledge is formalized first and its implementation arranged afterwards. The means through which something can be defined and the means through which it can be exercised impose demands on one another.

A required productive relation may have no adequate representation in the available environment. A mathematical instrument may express it while the existing machinery has no way to maintain or transform its instances. An implementation attempt may then expose a missing contextual distinction or a conflict among the original commitments. Resolving the problem can require development of the conception, its formal instruments and its realizing systems together.

The empirical ground of this work includes the powers already available: hardware, technical systems, established domain knowledge, and the conditions under which each actually works. Their limitations are part of the problem, as are the possibilities they leave unrealized. The undertaking is primarily constructive. Its object is a new organization of powers under the laws, resources and knowledge we possess, rather than necessarily a new law of nature. New scientific knowledge can enlarge those resources; substantial epistemic and technical creation can also proceed by making new use of what already exists.

Epistemic technē enters here as productive means: methods of distinguishing, representing, relating, deriving and constructing through which the relevant work becomes possible. The issue is not whether an instrument is abstract, but what it enables us to constitute and do. A richer treatment of identity can make a previously incoherent composition meaningful; an appropriate algebra can make a family of transformations composable; a formal account of context can determine when a result can be transported into another use. Their operative value depends on their substantive interpretation and realization, not on the prestige of their mathematical vocabulary.

Some requirements can be met by adapting or integrating existing instruments. Others may call for new constructions. Neither novelty nor reuse is the governing measure. The question is whether the necessary relationship has been supplied, and whether the apparatus can exercise it faithfully.

The resulting development may reach representational substrates, execution mechanisms and the architecture through which state and transformation coexist. A new level of abstraction can require new kinds of operative object; those objects may require powers that a present system does not offer. The appropriate response may be a local extension or a reconstruction of deeper foundations. The scope of intervention follows the responsibility, not an allegiance to current infrastructure or a presumption that all infrastructure must be replaced.

This is why current implementations cannot delimit the subject. A first system may make a particular class of objects and conditions operable while leaving much deeper organizations unavailable. The achievement is real at its scope. The general industrial direction concerns continuing expansion of the instruments and realizing powers through which further scopes can be constituted.

The probabilistic engine contributes to this development without standing outside it as its sovereign author. It can help articulate distinctions, explore constructions, connect bodies of material and produce parts of the technical realization. The work must still yield the epistemic and operative means the proposed capability requires. Once constituted, those means can enable further constructive work—including work that was previously too difficult or expensive to organize.

The reciprocal movement is therefore not only corrective. It can be expansive: better epistemic instruments make new machinery possible; new machinery makes further epistemic and productive work practicable. Each passage contains work of its own. The possibility of their reinforcement is important precisely because it is something to develop, not a benefit obtained by merely placing powerful components together.

### 6. Fallibility remains a responsibility of the machine

The causal constitution of a system also changes where its failure must be understood. A model can produce an unsuitable result, but “the model made an error” does not complete the industrial account. The relevant question is why that contribution, under those conditions, was permitted to acquire those consequences.

Its task may have been underdefined. The representation may have omitted a needed distinction. The assigned operation may have exceeded the constructor's established capability. A result may have been used outside the context that gave it meaning. The defect may lie in the conception, the realization or their composition. A final checker cannot supply all the knowledge and constructive means those failures expose as missing.

Probabilistic error can identify a mechanism of failure; it is not an independent category of responsibility to which the machine's designers can transfer the problem. The nature and limits of a constituent belong among the conditions for its productive use. If the machine relies on an uncertain contribution as though it established more than it does, that reliance is itself part of the design.

The point is not that formal construction abolishes fallibility. It locates responsibility for an undertaking in the constituted apparatus and its operation. Even a fully explicit arrangement can be inadequate; its repair must reach the actual cause of that inadequacy. Stronger engines can improve particular contributions, but their improvement cannot substitute for investigating the role those contributions have been assigned.

The premise of a feared scenario therefore belongs inside the inquiry. An indefinitely extended chain of inference given authority over critical operations is a designed arrangement, not a fate imposed by probabilistic computation. To assume it as the inevitable future and debate only the consequences is to decide on a jump without a parachute before asking how to land. The constructive question precedes that predicament: what role should this power occupy, and what apparatus can discharge the undertaking? Rejecting the premise leaves that work to be done; it does not make it unnecessary.

## Part III — The industrial meaning of formal construction

### 7. From technical accomplishment to a productive order

A capable machine is not yet an industrial revolution. Its existence establishes a productive possibility at a particular scope. Industrialization concerns the establishment of productive powers on a scale and in arrangements through which they become a continuing basis of economic and social life.

That passage involves more than repeating a successful operation. Capacities must be reproducible without their meaning being reinvented on each occasion; they must be maintained, combined with other capacities and made available for further development. Their establishment changes the organization of work: which responsibilities can be embodied in machinery, which require further expertise, which contributions can be transferred, and which forms of cooperation become possible.

The economic significance is not confined to producing an existing result more cheaply. A new productive means can make an activity practicable that could not previously sustain itself, or make a class of product available that the earlier organization could not supply. It can also change the work needed to develop subsequent capacities. These are changes in the constitution of production, not only in its rate.

Popper's “Epistemology and Industrialization” helps place that claim at its proper scale. He connects industrial development with an epistemic orientation: the conviction that new knowledge can alter humanity's material circumstances, given organized inquiry and constructive means. His account treats industrial civilization as a mode of life as well as a form of production, and scientific and technical development as interacting rather than as a completed theory followed by passive application.[^industrialization]

For the present argument, the important relationship is between what a civilization undertakes to understand and what it becomes able to construct. That relationship acquires historical force through actual instruments, practices and institutions. A philosophical conviction alone does not build an industry. Neither does an inventory of machines explain how the knowledge and organization making them possible came into existence.

The present opportunity concerns a further development of this relationship. We possess extensive knowledge and powerful technologies, but their coexistence does not constitute all the relationships through which they could become jointly productive. Existing fields frequently rely on capacities distributed among practitioners, instruments and institutions. New combinations can demand subjects and connections that none of the contributing arrangements had to supply on its own.

Making such relationships formal and operable can therefore be productive development in its own right. It can preserve and extend capabilities that otherwise have to be reconstructed through each new undertaking. It can also expose exactly where an apparent capacity depends on an unmade distinction, an unavailable instrument or an unsupported transformation.

The industrial ambition is to develop these powers as a growing, reusable basis of production. Its wider realization will depend on how they are sustained, distributed and incorporated into economic and institutional life. Those questions remain substantial. They are the reason the claim exceeds a programme of task automation.

### 8. Why the proposed revolution is formal

The term *formal* identifies where a central part of the new productive work must occur: in the constitution of subjects and possible transformations, the development of their laws of composition, and the correspondence through which those constructions acquire operative reality. Earlier industry already possessed knowledge, form and causal machinery. The proposed expansion concerns the depth and range of knowledge that can be made operative, and our capacity to develop the means for doing so.

Probabilistic programmability changes both the feasibility and the return of that effort. It supplies a means of undertaking parts of the symbolic work required to develop a formal organization. It also supplies transformations that the resulting organization can make specifically available and consequential. The effort of definition can therefore contribute directly to a productive apparatus, while the new computational power can help undertake the effort itself.

This gives formal construction two connected industrial returns. The first is the capacity to produce within a defined class of work. The second is a stock of productive means from which further capacities can be developed: objects whose natures have been adequately constituted, instruments for relating and transforming them, and mechanisms capable of realizing those relationships. Their accumulation changes what must be done anew.

Where a realizing environment already supports the forms a new definition requires, those forms need not receive a fresh implementation on every occasion. The new effort can concentrate on the particular domain's objects, relationships and productive responsibilities. Where a new responsibility exceeds those forms, the missing distinction or operative power becomes a further problem of epistemic and technical development. Reuse and creation cooperate: established means carry what they actually support, while the new undertaking determines what remains to be constituted.

That is a substantive change in the development of productive capability itself. An advance in a common instrument can enlarge the class of machines that can be built with it. An adequate realization can make a previously theoretical relationship available for use. A newly constituted domain can expose a more general need whose solution becomes useful elsewhere. These gains depend on establishing which meanings and powers transfer into the next setting; proximity of vocabulary or compatibility of data is insufficient.

The resulting expansion need not remain at one level of abstraction. When the means for constructing a class of productive systems themselves become adequately understood and operable, parts of their construction can become a further productive responsibility. The object of industry is then not only a succession of products, but the development of the capabilities that make further classes of product possible. This is a direction of work, with real requirements at each passage, rather than a promise of effortless or unlimited self-improvement.

The distinction from an engine-centered future is now substantive. The engine's expansion is an advance in an available constructive power. The Formal Industrial Revolution concerns the development of the productive worlds and apparatuses through which such powers, together with others, become capable of defined classes of work. Its central achievement is not a more impressive attempt at an undertaking, but the constitution of the capability to undertake it.

**Causality takes center stage because the relationships through which production occurs must become objects of construction, not responsibilities deferred to the next inference.** Probability retains an important productive role. The decisive question is what has been made possible through the whole, and how its constituents make that possibility real.

The longer horizon follows from this distinction. If the twenty-second century is to inherit substantially enlarged productive powers, the present one must develop more than increasingly capable engines. It must develop the epistemic instruments, formal organizations and technical embodiments through which those powers can become a productive order. That is a programme of deliberate construction. Its opening has become visible; its execution remains ours.

## Further development — questions and directions opened by this seed

The account above establishes an initial direction and its principal relationships. It does not yet provide a complete theory of industrialization, a history of previous industrial revolutions or a specification for all future productive systems. The next work should deepen the following questions rather than treat their names as answers.

**The unit and threshold of industrialization.** A domain of possible work, an economic activity, a firm and an industrial sector need not share a boundary. We need to define how a constituted capability enters those organizations, and what distinguishes an industrializable machine from an industrialized activity or a civilizational transformation. Reproducibility, extensibility and wider establishment are initial coordinates, not a finished criterion.

**The counter-position and its strongest claims.** The entity-centered conception has been examined as an argument, not established as a uniform description of an industry. Further work should distinguish actual claims about general intelligence, autonomy, probability and surrounding infrastructure; determine who asserts what; and develop our criticism against their strongest formulations. Claims that changes in infrastructure contribute more than particular changes in training require specific evidence and meaningful measures. No such quantitative verdict is established here.

**The nature and adequacy of causal domain models.** We need a fuller treatment of how formally constituted productive relations connect with knowledge about external circumstances. Identity, observation, interpretation, causal explanation and operative transition must receive their appropriate treatment without being collapsed into one notion of a model. The division of work between this account and the deeper domain-modelling definition should preserve that depth without duplicating it.

**The reach of generative capability.** The distinction between new instances and new kinds of work opens questions of discovery, extension and the creation of further productive means. Which extensions can a machine construct within its established powers? Which require new epistemic instruments or new realizing capacities? Claims of coverage, termination, resources and adequacy must be developed for their actual scopes rather than inferred from indefinite compositional possibility.

**The historical and epistemic–industrial bridge.** Popper supplies one route into the relationship between epistemic orientation and industrial development. We still need selected, closely developed historical cases that establish their actual mechanisms: the instruments involved, their passage into productive means, and the reciprocal demands through which knowledge and practice changed. The purpose is to illuminate the nature of the present undertaking, not force all previous revolutions into a single causal formula.

**The institutions and technical foundations of further capability.** The civilizational claim requires an account of how productive knowledge is maintained, combined, transmitted and made available for further construction. It may demand new representational environments and deeper technical foundations where current systems cannot support the required objects and operations. The future architecture must follow those requirements; a provisional name for a technology is not yet its definition.

**The responsibility of the present century.** The twenty-second-century horizon should become more than an appeal to long-term ambition. We need to identify which cumulative foundations, forms of expertise, institutions and productive means would make the intended expansion possible, what can be undertaken from present capacities, and where the unresolved work lies. That development should distinguish what we can already begin, what requires further discovery, and what future choices it should leave open.
