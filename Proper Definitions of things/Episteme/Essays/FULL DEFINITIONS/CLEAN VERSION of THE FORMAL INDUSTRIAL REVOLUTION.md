The loss of our grip gathered momentum in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, with the resurgence of radical metaphysical system-building in Fichte, Schelling and, above all, Hegel, and the countervailing anti-metaphysical radicalism of Comte and the positivists who followed. At their dogmatic extremes, one insulated its systems against criticism and prescribed what reality must be; the other prescribed what inquiry was permitted to ask. Philosophy’s constructive responsibilities were increasingly displaced by the contest between speculative authority and epistemic prohibition. These were not the only sources of deterioration, but they furnished two of its most influential roots—roots whose ramifications would spread far beyond their original antagonism and become more deeply entrenched before the dawn of the twentieth century, and especially after.

In the twentieth century, these and related currents increasingly claimed authority not merely over particular theories, but over the constructive ambitions of our intellectual tradition. System-building became suspect, together with the epistemic confidence that we could create better knowledge in all directions, bring our explanations closer to reality and develop new means of turning understanding into productive power. Verificationist prohibitions, operationalist restrictions and explanatory reductionism supplied different warrants for narrowing that ambition; the limitations of particular instruments were promoted into limits upon legitimate inquiry. Wittgenstein’s injunction to silence and the linguistic programmes of dissolution gave withdrawal its own prestige: constructing an explanation could be treated as a confusion to be cured rather than an undertaking to be developed. As these commitments acquired schools, disciples and institutional authority, the rejection of constructive ambition could pass for intellectual sophistication. 

Later, framework relativism and anti-realist currents within postmodernism and deconstruction turned the conjectural character of knowledge against the pursuit of objective truth itself. Where these positions prevailed, the tradition was being trained to distrust precisely those powers through which it had enlarged the world of human possibility. The consequences extended beyond theories: discrediting the creation of new conceptual systems also weakened the development of instruments through which discoveries could be connected, knowledge made operable and new industries conceived. Instrumentalism supplied this retreat with its most effective accommodation to achievement. Calculation, prediction and practical performance could flourish while the demand for explanatory depth was dismissed as an unnecessary encumbrance; statistical significance and predictive fit could pass for causal knowledge without supplying its substance. By the century’s close, the accumulated means of inquiry were increasingly invoked against the intellectual freedom required to reconstruct them. Rigor could be identified with fidelity to prescribed procedures, while the creation of more adequate procedures became suspect. The civilizational injury was not simply that certain questions went unanswered: it was the erosion of our confidence and responsibility to create the means of answering them. Instruments fashioned to extend our powers were elevated into authorities over their further development; their inherited limits were becoming prescriptions for our future. By the end of the 20th century, anything that can be called institutionalized was fundamentally epistemically pesimistic, and suspect of any serious creative attempt.

That is not to say that the tradition itself didn't fight this, is just that it lost the battle for its institutionalization.
